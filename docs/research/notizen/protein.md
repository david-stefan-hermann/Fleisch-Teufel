# Protein requirements of healthy adults (resistance training, muscle gain, fat loss with muscle retention): evidence as of 2026

Reading notes for the report writer:

- "Verified" means I read the abstract on PubMed or the full text. "Secondary" means I only have a summary by a third party. Anything I could not check is listed under Gaps.
- All values are g protein per kg body mass per day (g/kg/d) unless stated otherwise. FFM = fat-free mass, LBM = lean body mass, RT = resistance training, CI = 95 % confidence interval, HDI = highest density interval (Bayesian).
- Typography: no dashes as punctuation, ranges are written "a to b", negative numbers use a hyphen.

## 1. Is 1.6 g/kg/d a plateau? Morton 2018, its critiques, and newer dose-response meta-analyses (Tagawa, Nunes, Refalo)

### Takeaway

The "1.6 plateau" is a point estimate from a statistically non-significant, unweighted breakpoint model (p = 0.079) with a confidence interval of 1.03 to 2.20 g/kg/d, and the authors themselves say it "may be prudent to recommend ~2.2 g/kg/d" for people who want to maximise gains. Newer analyses agree on strongly diminishing returns above roughly 1.3 to 1.6 g/kg/d but do not show a hard ceiling there: the marginal gain between 1.6 and about 2.0 to 2.2 g/kg/d is small (order of a few hundred grams of lean mass over a training study) and uncertain, not proven to be zero.

### Cited Findings

- Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med 52(6):376-384, systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression: 49 RCTs, 1863 participants, RT of at least 6 weeks. Protein supplementation added 1RM strength +2.49 kg (CI 0.64 to 4.33), FFM +0.30 kg (0.09 to 0.52), fibre CSA +310 µm² (51 to 570), mid-femur CSA +7.2 mm² (0.20 to 14.30). (verified, full text): [Morton 2018, PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/), [full text PMC5867436](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5867436), doi 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608
- Morton 2018 breakpoint, quoted from the full text: "Biphasic regression (42 study arms, 723 participants) explained more variation than a linear regression between the change in FFM and daily protein intake (break point=1.62 (1.03, 2.20) g/kg/day, slope=1.75, R2=0.19, df=36) and is presented as a segmental regression despite not being statistically significant (p=0.079)". Intakes in the data ranged from 0.9 to 2.4 g/kg/d; the second slope was fixed at zero; every study arm was one unweighted point; the authors call it an "unadjusted plateau": [Morton 2018 full text](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5867436)
- Morton 2018, authors' own recommendation: "Given that the CI of this estimate spanned from 1.03 to 2.20, it may be prudent to recommend ~2.2 g protein/kg/d for those seeking to maximise resistance training-induced gains in FFM.": [Morton 2018 full text](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5867436)
- Morton 2018 moderators: the supplementation effect on FFM fell with age (-0.01 kg per year, CI -0.02 to -0.00, p = 0.002) and was larger in resistance-trained people (+0.75 kg, CI 0.09 to 1.40, p = 0.03); only 10 studies were in trained participants. Mean baseline intake was about 1.4 g/kg/d and the average supplement about 35 g/d: [Morton 2018 full text](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5867436)
- Critique (non-peer-reviewed, Greg Nuckols, Stronger by Science, Dec 2024, updated July 2025): the breakpoint is not significant, its CI spans nearly the whole studied range, the model assumes a zero slope after the break, 39 of 49 studies used untrained people. In the trained subset the pooled effect was +1.05 kg (0.61 to 1.50) versus +0.15 kg (-0.02 to 0.31) in untrained, and several trained studies (Cribb 2007, Hoffman 2007 and 2009, Kerksick 2006) had control intakes around 1.6 g/kg/d and still gained more with added protein. His reanalysis of the Nunes 2022 dataset (50 studies) gives a decreasing slope (effect size per g/kg: 0.88 below 1.5, 0.38 at 1.5 to 1.99, 0.19 at 2.0 or more) and a best-estimate breakpoint near 2.0 g/kg/d (plausible 1.7 to 2.35, a judgment without a formal interval, one outlier study excluded). He also concludes that 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/d "captures most potential gains". (secondary, read through a page summary): [Stronger by Science, Protein Science Updated](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/protein-science/)
- Tagawa et al. 2021, Nutr Rev 79(1):66-75, dose-response meta-analysis with spline model: 105 articles, 5402 participants, RCTs with and without RT. LBM rose across 0.5 to 3.5 g/kg/d; the slope was +0.39 kg (CI 0.36 to 0.41) per 0.1 g/kg/d below 1.3 g/kg/d and +0.12 kg (0.11 to 0.14) per 0.1 g/kg/d above 1.3. With RT: 0.06 kg (0.03 to 0.08) per 0.1 g/kg from 0.52 to 1.30 and 0.08 kg (0.06 to 0.09) from 1.31 to 3.50 g/kg/d. Authors: the rate of increase "rapidly diminished after 1.3 g/kg BW/d was exceeded, and resistance training markedly suppressed this decline." (verified, full text): [Tagawa 2021, PubMed 33300582](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33300582/), [PMC7727026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7727026)
- Critique of Tagawa 2021 (secondary, from search summaries of Menno Henselmans' article): only 4 of the 105 included articles examined a dose-response under identical conditions, so the between-study slope is confounded; he argues the analysis does not show a need for intakes up to about 3 g/kg/d and concedes that the true maximum is unknown: [Henselmans, Optimal Protein Intake is 1.5g/lb, says new study](https://mennohenselmans.com/optimal-protein-intake)
- Tagawa et al. 2022, Sports Med Open 8:110: 82 articles (69 in the spline model). With RT, muscle strength rose +0.72 % (CI 0.40 to 1.04) per 0.1 g/kg/d up to 1.5 g/kg/d, with no further gain above; without RT protein had no effect on strength (MD 0.13 %, -1.53 to 1.79). (verified, abstract): [Tagawa 2022, PubMed 36057893](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36057893/), doi 10.1186/s40798-022-00508-w
- Nunes et al. 2022, J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 13(2):795-810, systematic review with three-level meta-analysis: 74 RCTs in healthy, non-obese adults. Higher protein with RT: LBM SMD 0.22 (CI 0.14 to 0.30), 62 studies, moderate certainty. The effect was significant at 1.6 g/kg/d or more in adults under 65 and at 1.2 to 1.59 g/kg/d in adults of 65 and over. Bench press SMD 0.18 (0.03 to 0.33); lower-body strength at 1.6 g/kg/d or more SMD 0.40 (low certainty, 19 studies; the interval printed in the abstract, 0.09 to 0.35, does not contain the point estimate, so one of the numbers is a typo). Conclusion: "small additional gains". (verified, abstract only): [Nunes 2022, PubMed 35187864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/), doi 10.1002/jcsm.12922
- Hudson et al. 2020, Adv Nutr 11(3):548-558, 18 studies, intake above the RDA versus at the RDA: lean mass +0.32 kg (CI 0.01 to 0.64) overall; +0.36 kg (0.06 to 0.67) under energy restriction; +0.77 kg (0.23 to 1.31) with RT (n = 3 studies); +0.08 kg (-0.59 to 0.75) without a stressor. The RDA looks adequate only for people who neither train nor diet. (verified, abstract): [Hudson 2020, PubMed 31794597](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31794597/)
- Refalo, Trexler, Helms 2025, Strength and Conditioning Journal, systematic review with Bayesian meta-regression in energy-restricted, resistance-trained, non-obese adults (31 eligible studies, 29 extracted; 455 men, 274 women; mean age about 25; 1 to 21 weeks; mean deficit 557 kcal/d). A linear model fitted best: slope per 1 g/kg body mass β = 0.07 (95 % HDI -0.01 to 0.14, 97 % probability of a positive slope), per 1 g/kg FFM β = 0.06 (0.01 to 0.12, 99 %). No plateau was detectable within 0.8 to 3.2 g/kg/d, but the whole span corresponds only to a "small" effect size difference. (verified, full text): [Refalo 2025, open PDF (AUT repository)](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content), doi [10.1519/SSC.0000000000000888](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000888)
- The "plateau" side as stated in 2025: a McMaster University commentary says meta-analyses pooling dozens of RT studies consistently show that benefits of protein supplementation plateau at about 1.6 g/kg/d (opinion piece, secondary): [McMaster, 2025 was the year protein jumped the shark](https://news.mcmaster.ca/analysis-2025-was-the-year-protein-jumped-the-shark/)

### Inferences

- The claim "above 1.5 to 1.6 g/kg/d there is no proven additional effect" is half right. Right: no single analysis shows a statistically robust extra gain above 1.6 in mixed populations, and strength seems to stop responding near 1.5 (Tagawa 2022). Wrong as a statement of a ceiling: the only formal breakpoint has a CI up to 2.2, was not significant, and its authors recommend up to 2.2; trained subgroups, spline models and the Bayesian deficit analysis all show positive (if shallow) slopes above 1.6.
- "Not proven" is mostly a power problem: the expected marginal effect is small. Total effect of supplementation in Morton is +0.30 kg FFM; Nunes' SMD 0.22 is on the order of half a kilogram (Stronger by Science converts it to about 0.6 kg, secondary). The step from 1.6 to 2.0 or 2.2 is a fraction of that.
- Tagawa's slopes per 0.1 g/kg/d cannot be multiplied over a wide range (0.39 kg per 0.1 g/kg would mean several kg of LBM per 1 g/kg). Treat them as evidence of direction and of the bend near 1.3, not as a calculator.
- For an app: 1.6 g/kg/d is a defensible default (most of the benefit, low cost); 2.0 to 2.2 g/kg/d is a defensible "maximise" setting, not "unnecessary". Describing 2.0 as useless is not supported; describing it as clearly better is not supported either.

### Gaps

- Nunes 2022 full text could not be retrieved (Europe PMC returned an error); the category effects (below 1.2: g = -0.14; 1.2 to 1.59: 0.17; 1.6 or more: 0.30) and the statement that its continuous model was linear are only from Stronger by Science.
- Stronger by Science was read through an automatic page summary; its numbers (trained versus untrained effects, reanalysis slopes, breakpoint 2.0) are not checked against the article text line by line and are not peer reviewed.
- No new peer-reviewed dose-response meta-analysis for muscle gain in energy balance or surplus from 2023 to 2026 was found beyond those listed; I cannot rule out that one exists.
- Henselmans' original article ("0.82 g/lb is the maximum", about 1.8 g/kg) could not be fetched (HTTP 403); his position is only known from search summaries.
- Whether Tagawa's authors have industry affiliations was not verified.

## 2. What do IAAO studies say, and how solid is the RDA of 0.8 g/kg/d (nitrogen balance)?

### Takeaway

Indicator amino acid oxidation (IAAO) studies put the average requirement of resistance-trained people at about 1.5 to 2.0 g/kg/d with upper 95 % bounds of about 1.9 to 2.4 g/kg/d, and put sedentary adults at about 0.9 (safe level about 1.2) instead of the nitrogen-balance values 0.66 and 0.8. They are small (5 to 8 participants), acute, whole-body measurements that may be biased upward by high habitual intake, so they support "1.6 is not the ceiling" but do not prove that eating more builds more muscle.

### Cited Findings

- Bandegan et al. 2017, J Nutr 147(5):850-857: 8 male bodybuilders (83.9 kg, 13 % fat), non-training day, 42 experiments at 0.1 to 3.5 g/kg/d. Estimated average requirement 1.7 g/kg/d, upper 95 % CI ("RDA") 2.2 g/kg/d, about 2.6 times the IOM values. (verified, abstract): [Bandegan 2017, PubMed 28179492](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28179492/), doi 10.3945/jn.116.236331
- Mazzulla et al. 2020, J Nutr 150(3):505-511: 7 resistance-trained men (80 ± 9 kg, 11 ± 5 % fat, habitual intake 2.3 ± 0.6 g/kg/d), after whole-body resistance exercise, intakes 0.20 to 3.00 g/kg/d. Plateau in phenylalanine oxidation at 2.00 g/kg/d (CI 1.62 to 2.38, r² = 0.64) and in net balance at 2.01 g/kg/d (CI 1.44 to 2.57). Authors: about 2.0 g/kg/d "is at the upper range of current general protein recommendations for athletes". (verified, abstract): [Mazzulla 2020, PubMed 31618421](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31618421/), doi 10.1093/jn/nxz249
- Malowany et al. 2019, Med Sci Sports Exerc 51(4):798-804: 8 resistance-trained women, luteal phase, after exercise. Breakpoint 1.49 ± 0.44 g/kg/d (oxidation) and 1.53 ± 0.32 g/kg/d (net balance); the safe intake lies at the upper end of the ACSM range of 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/d. (verified, abstract): [Malowany 2019, PubMed 30395050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30395050/), doi 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001832
- Kato et al. 2016, PLoS One 11(6):e0157406: 6 male endurance athletes after a 20 km run. Average requirement 1.65 g/kg/d, recommended intake 1.83 g/kg/d. (verified, abstract): [Kato 2016, PubMed 27322029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27322029/), doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0157406
- Humayun et al. 2007, Am J Clin Nutr 86(4):995-1002: 8 healthy young men, IAAO mean requirement 0.93 g/kg/d and population-safe 1.2 g/kg/d, 41 % and 50 % above the current EAR 0.66 and RDA 0.80. (verified, abstract): [Humayun 2007, PubMed 17921376](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17921376/)
- Elango et al. 2010, Curr Opin Clin Nutr Metab Care 13(1):52-57: re-analysing the existing nitrogen-balance data with two-phase linear regression instead of a single line gives 0.91 and 0.99 g/kg/d (mean and safe), close to the IAAO values; the authors call current recommendations underestimated. (verified, abstract): [Elango 2010, PubMed 19841581](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19841581/)
- Carbone and Pasiakos 2019, Nutrients 11(5):1136: the RDA is the minimum to prevent deficiency, not an optimum; higher intakes benefit muscle and health. (verified, abstract): [Carbone 2019, PubMed 31121843](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31121843/)
- Critique of IAAO: Tinline-Goodfellow et al. 2020, Front Nutr 7:55: 5 trained men with a habitual intake of 2.69 g/kg/d were moved to 1.2 g/kg/d; on day 1 phenylalanine oxidation was about 72 % higher and net balance 25 % lower, and adaptation took 3 to 5 days. A high habitual intake can therefore inflate IAAO estimates obtained after short adaptation. (verified, abstract): [Tinline-Goodfellow 2020, PubMed 32391374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32391374/), doi 10.3389/fnut.2020.00055
- IAAO values per kg FFM (secondary, Stronger by Science): Bandegan about 2.0 average and 2.5 upper bound g/kg FFM; Malowany 1.49 g/kg body mass = 2.13 g/kg FFM, 95th percentile 1.93 g/kg body mass; Mazzulla 2.0 g/kg body mass = 2.35 g/kg FFM, upper bound 2.38 (2.9 g/kg FFM): [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/protein-science/)
- Without a stressor the RDA holds up in outcome trials: Hudson 2020 found no lean mass advantage of more than the RDA when people neither trained nor dieted (+0.08 kg, CI -0.59 to 0.75): [Hudson 2020, PubMed 31794597](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31794597/)

### Inferences

- Four independent IAAO studies in trained people give average requirements of 1.5 (women), 1.65 (endurance), 1.7 and 2.0 g/kg/d. If the "RDA logic" (cover 97.5 % of people) is applied, the target is about 1.9 to 2.4 g/kg/d. That is the strongest physiological argument that 1.6 is closer to an average requirement than to a ceiling.
- Limits: n of 5 to 8, mostly men, single test days, whole-body protein synthesis (not muscle), amino acid mixtures instead of food, participants with habitual intakes of 2.3 to 2.7 g/kg/d. Maximal whole-body anabolism on a test day is not the same as more hypertrophy over months.
- The 0.8 g/kg/d reference value should be presented in the app as a minimum for sedentary adults (IAAO suggests even that is about 20 to 50 % too low), not as "balanced".

### Gaps

- A published commentary on Mazzulla 2020 (Reidy, mentioned in a search snippet) raising design issues was not read.
- No IAAO study in people in an energy deficit, in older trained adults, or in women above the luteal phase design was found.
- The percent values per kg FFM are from a secondary source.

## 3. What do position stands and reference bodies recommend?

### Takeaway

Sports nutrition bodies converge on 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/d for training adults (ISSN 1.4 to 2.0, ACSM/AND/DC and DGE sports group 1.2 to 2.0, bodybuilding reviews 1.6 to 2.2), with higher values when dieting (1.8 to 2.4 g/kg, or 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg lean mass for lean athletes). Public health bodies (DGE/D-A-CH 0.8, EFSA 0.83, older adults 1.0 to 1.2) define minimum adequacy for the general population, and none of them sets an upper limit.

### Cited Findings

- ISSN position stand, Jäger et al. 2017, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:20: 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/d is sufficient for most exercising individuals; 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg/d may be needed to maximise lean mass retention in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods; intakes above 3.0 g/kg/d may have positive effects on body composition in resistance-trained people; per dose 0.25 g/kg or 20 to 40 g, every 3 to 4 hours. (verified, abstract): [Jäger 2017, PubMed 28642676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/), doi 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8
- ACSM, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada joint position, Thomas, Erdman, Burke 2016, Med Sci Sports Exerc 48(3):543-568: [Thomas 2016, PubMed 26891166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/). Its range of 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/d is quoted in the Malowany abstract: [Malowany 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30395050/)
- Phillips and Van Loon 2011, J Sports Sci 29 Suppl 1:S29-38: 1.3 to 1.8 g/kg/d for athletes, 1.8 to 2.0 g/kg/d during energy restriction; experienced athletes may need less. (verified, abstract): [Phillips 2011, PubMed 22150425](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22150425/)
- Iraki et al. 2019, Sports 7(7):154, off-season bodybuilding review: 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg/d, 0.40 to 0.55 g/kg per meal over 3 to 6 meals, fat 0.5 to 1.5 g/kg/d. (verified, abstract): [Iraki 2019, PubMed 31247944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/)
- Helms, Aragon, Fitschen 2014, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:20, contest preparation review: weight loss of 0.5 to 1 % per week; "most but not all bodybuilders will respond best to consuming 2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean body mass per day of protein, 15-30% of calories from fat"; 3 to 6 meals with 0.4 to 0.5 g/kg per meal around training. (verified, abstract): [Helms 2014b, PubMed 24864135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/), doi 10.1186/1550-2783-11-20
- Helms et al. 2014, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 24(2):127-138, systematic review (6 studies, 13 groups) in lean resistance-trained athletes in a deficit: 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg FFM, scaled up with the severity of the deficit and leanness. (verified, abstract): [Helms 2014a, PubMed 24092765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)
- Schoenfeld and Aragon 2018, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 15:10: 0.4 g/kg per meal across at least 4 meals to reach 1.6 g/kg/d; up to 0.55 g/kg per meal for the upper daily intake of 2.2 g/kg/d. (verified, abstract): [Schoenfeld 2018, PubMed 29497353](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/)
- Per-meal dose response: Moore et al. 2015, J Gerontol A 70(1):57-62, retrospective breakpoint analysis: myofibrillar protein synthesis plateaus at 0.24 ± 0.06 g/kg per meal in young men and 0.40 ± 0.19 g/kg in older men (0.25 and 0.60 g/kg lean mass). (verified, abstract): [Moore 2015, PubMed 25056502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)
- Trommelen et al. 2023, Cell Rep Med 4(12):101324: 100 g of protein in one meal gave a larger and longer (more than 12 h) anabolic response than 25 g, with a negligible effect on amino acid oxidation; there is no practical per-meal cap. (verified, abstract): [Trommelen 2023, PubMed 38118410](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/)
- Timing: Schoenfeld, Aragon, Krieger 2013, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 10:53, meta-analysis: the timing effect disappeared after adjusting for covariates; total daily protein intake was the strongest predictor of hypertrophy. (verified, abstract): [Schoenfeld 2013, PubMed 24299050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/). Wirth et al. 2020, J Nutr 150(6):1443-1460: 65 studies, 2907 participants, LBM +0.62 kg (CI 0.36 to 0.88) in adults and +0.46 kg (0.23 to 0.70) in older adults with more protein, no effect of timing: [Wirth 2020, PubMed 32232404](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32232404/)
- DGE/D-A-CH reference values, Richter et al. 2019, Ann Nutr Metab 74(3):242-250: adults under 65: 0.8 g/kg/d; 65 and over: 1.0 g/kg/d as an estimated value; derived from nitrogen balance. (verified, abstract): [Richter 2019, PubMed 30904906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30904906/)
- DGE working group sports nutrition, König et al. 2020, Ernährungs Umschau 67(7):132-139: about 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/d depending on training status and goal, from normal food; temporarily higher intakes are possible (for example weight loss with muscle retention); data for intakes up to 3 g/kg are insufficient; no clear advantage of animal protein. (from search snippets of the position paper, full text not read): [König 2020, Ernährungs Umschau PDF](https://www.ernaehrungs-umschau.de/fileadmin/Ernaehrungs-Umschau/pdfs/pdf_2020/07_20/EU07_2020_PR_DGE_Sport_eng.pdf), doi 10.4455/eu.2020.039
- EFSA 2012 (doi 10.2903/j.efsa.2012.2557): average requirement 0.66, population reference intake 0.83 g/kg/d; "Intakes up to twice the PRI are regularly consumed from mixed diets by some physically active and healthy adults in Europe and are considered safe"; no tolerable upper intake level because data are insufficient. (secondary, news report quoting the opinion): [FoodNavigator on EFSA 2012](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2012/02/10/EFSA-sets-recommended-EU-protein-intakes-at-0.8g-kg-per-day)
- Older adults: PROT-AGE, Bauer et al. 2013, J Am Med Dir Assoc 14(8):542-559: 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/d over 65, 1.2 or more when active, 1.2 to 1.5 with acute or chronic illness, exception severe kidney disease (eGFR under 30) without dialysis: [Bauer 2013, PubMed 23867520](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/). ESPEN expert group, Deutz et al. 2014, Clin Nutr 33(6):929-936: healthy older adults 1.0 to 1.2, malnourished or ill 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/d: [Deutz 2014, PubMed 24814383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814383/). Morton, Traylor, Weijs, Phillips 2018, Curr Opin Crit Care: older people should eat at least 1.2 g/kg/d in boluses of 0.40 g/kg: [Morton 2018b, PubMed 29389741](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29389741/)
- Examine.com guide (read only through a mirror in search results, not the live page): 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg/d for athletes and active adults who want to gain muscle, 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/d if overweight, up to 3.1 g/kg/d may help lean lifters in a deficit: [Examine, Optimal Protein Intake Guide](https://examine.com/guides/protein-intake/)

### Inferences

- The app's presets map onto recognised sources: 1.0 = slightly above DGE/EFSA minimum; 1.6 = middle of every sports range; 2.0 = upper end of ISSN, ACSM and DGE sports range and within Iraki's 1.6 to 2.2. None of the bodies describes 2.0 as excessive.
- ISSN and Helms disagree in units: Helms gives 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of lean mass, the ISSN abstract repeats the same numbers per kg body mass. For a man with 15 % body fat, 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg FFM equals about 2.0 to 2.6 g/kg body mass. Use the FFM reading.
- "EFSA says up to 1.7 g/kg/d is safe" is an observation about commonly eaten amounts, not an upper limit; EFSA explicitly set none.
- Meal distribution matters little compared with the daily total (Schoenfeld 2013, Wirth 2020, Trommelen 2023); a per-meal target is optional. If shown: about 0.4 g/kg per meal, 0.4 to 0.6 g/kg for older adults.

### Gaps

- IOC consensus statements on sports nutrition were not researched.
- Stokes et al. 2018 (Nutrients 10(2):180, [PubMed 29414855](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29414855/)): the abstract contains no numbers; its specific recommendations were not verified.
- The ACSM/AND/DC paper, the EFSA opinion and the DGE sports position were not read in full text; the numbers come from citing papers, a news report and search snippets.
- The live Examine page returned HTTP 429; the numbers come from a mirror and may be out of date.

## 4. Does the protein need rise in an energy deficit, and with leanness?

### Takeaway

Yes, with moderate certainty: controlled trials in deficits show clearly better lean mass retention at about 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg/d than at 0.8 to 1.2, and the 2025 Bayesian meta-regression finds a linear positive relation up to 3.2 g/kg/d that looks steeper in lean people; "no lean mass loss" is reached on average around 1.9 g/kg body mass (2.5 g/kg FFM). Whether 2.4 beats 1.6 is not settled (one direct trial found no difference), so the evidence supports raising the target when dieting, with the size of the extra benefit above about 2 g/kg uncertain.

### Cited Findings

- Refalo, Trexler, Helms 2025: slope per g/kg body mass 0.07 (HDI -0.01 to 0.14, 97 % probability positive; 56 outcomes, 27 studies), per g/kg FFM 0.06 (0.01 to 0.12, 99 %). The trend line crosses zero FFM change at 1.9 g/kg body mass or 2.5 g/kg FFM. Subgroups (per kg body mass): low body fat β = 0.14 (-0.04 to 0.31, 93 %), medium 0.10, high 0.04 (78 %); men 0.09 (0.00 to 0.19, 98 %), women 0.05 (-0.06 to 0.16, 81 %); deficit of 300 kcal or less 0.08, larger 0.04. Recommendation table: high risk of FFM loss or high importance: 1.9 to 3.2 g/kg body mass (2.5 to 4.2 g/kg FFM); moderate: at least 1.9; low: up to 1.9; "protein intake should inversely scale with BF%" (lean meaning under about 12 % in men, 20 % in women). Stated limits: "heterogeneity between studies renders our findings exploratory" (I² about 85 %), only 5 studies compared intakes directly, self-reported intake, single extractor, FFM is not muscle. (verified, full text): [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)
- Longland et al. 2016, Am J Clin Nutr 103(3):738-746, RCT: 40 young men, 4 weeks, about 40 % energy deficit, resistance plus high-intensity training 6 days a week, 4-compartment model. 2.4 versus 1.2 g/kg/d: LBM +1.2 ± 1.0 versus +0.1 ± 1.0 kg; fat mass -4.8 ± 1.6 versus -3.5 ± 1.4 kg. (verified, abstract): [Longland 2016, PubMed 26817506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)
- Mettler et al. 2010, Med Sci Sports Exerc 42(2):326-337, RCT: 20 resistance-trained athletes, 2 weeks at 60 % of habitual energy. About 2.3 g/kg/d (35 % of energy) versus 1.0 g/kg/d (15 %): lean mass loss -0.3 ± 0.3 versus -1.6 ± 0.3 kg (p = 0.006); total weight loss -1.5 versus -3.0 kg; fat loss did not differ. (verified, abstract): [Mettler 2010, PubMed 19927027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19927027/)
- Pasiakos et al. 2013, FASEB J 27(9):3837-3847, RCT: 39 adults, 21 days, 40 % deficit, 0.8 versus 1.6 versus 2.4 g/kg/d. The share of weight loss coming from FFM was lower at 1.6 and 2.4 than at the RDA; 2.4 gave no extra protection over 1.6. (verified, abstract): [Pasiakos 2013, PubMed 23739654](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23739654/)
- Hector and Phillips 2018, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 28(2):170-177: 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg/d for athletes losing weight, the position in the range depending on deficit size and training. (verified, abstract): [Hector 2018, PubMed 29182451](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182451/)
- Murphy and Koehler 2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports 32(1):125-137, meta-analysis: an energy deficit impairs lean mass gains from RT (effect size -0.57, p = 0.02) but not strength; a deficit of about 500 kcal/d is predicted to prevent lean mass gain. (verified, abstract): [Murphy 2022, PubMed 34623696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)
- General (mostly overweight, not necessarily training) dieters: Wycherley et al. 2012, Am J Clin Nutr 96(6):1281-1298, 24 trials, 1063 people, high versus standard protein energy-restricted diets: weight -0.79 kg (CI -1.50 to -0.08), fat mass -0.87 kg (-1.26 to -0.48), FFM +0.43 kg (0.09 to 0.78) retained, resting energy expenditure +595.5 kJ/d (67.0 to 1124.1): [Wycherley 2012, PubMed 23097268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23097268/). Krieger et al. 2006, Am J Clin Nutr 83(2):260-274, meta-regression of 87 studies: intake above 1.05 g/kg/d went with 0.60 kg more FFM retained, 1.21 kg in studies longer than 12 weeks: [Krieger 2006, PubMed 16469983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16469983/). Kokura et al. 2024, Clin Nutr ESPEN 63:417-426, 47 studies (n = 3218), adults with overweight or obesity losing weight: higher protein preserved muscle mass (SMD 0.75, CI 0.41 to 1.10); above 1.3 g/kg/d muscle mass tended to increase, below 1.0 g/kg/d the risk of decline rose: [Kokura 2024, PubMed 39002131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39002131/)
- Helms 2014 (2.3 to 3.1 g/kg FFM, scaling with leanness and deficit): [Helms 2014a, PubMed 24092765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/). Refalo et al. themselves note that this earlier recommendation rested on very few direct comparisons: [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content). Menno Henselmans published a critique along the same line (search snippet only): [Henselmans on Helms](https://mennohenselmans.com/eric-helms-protein/)

### Inferences

- Direction is consistent across designs: dieting raises the intake at which lean mass is protected. A deficit preset should be above the maintenance preset, so the app's 2.0 "diet / muscle retention" preset is on the supported side, and for lean, hard-training users 2.2 to 2.6 g/kg body mass (about 2.5 to 3.1 g/kg FFM) is defensible.
- Both sides in numbers: Pasiakos (1.6 = 2.4) is the best direct evidence for "1.6 is enough even in a deficit"; Longland and Mettler compared 2.3 to 2.4 against 1.0 to 1.2 and cannot say whether 1.6 would have done the same; Refalo's slope (0.07 SMD per g/kg) means going from 1.6 to 2.4 buys about 0.06 SMD, a small effect with an interval that touches zero.
- Leanness: the slope estimate is three times larger in the leanest subgroup than in the highest body fat subgroup, but the intervals overlap; "scale protein up as body fat goes down" is plausible and physiologically sensible, not proven.
- In people with obesity who diet without athletic goals, 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg actual weight (above 1.3 per Kokura) already captures the benefit.

### Gaps

- No long (over 6 months) RCT compares 1.6 with 2.2 or more in lean resistance-trained dieters.
- Longland's participant description (overweight, recreationally active) was not confirmed from the abstract.
- Evidence in women is weaker (Refalo: 81 % probability of a positive slope), and no female-only study above about 1.5 g/kg/d is known to Stronger by Science (secondary).

## 5. Do very high intakes (above about 2.2 g/kg/d) add anything? Satiety, thermic effect, protein leverage

### Takeaway

Trials at 3.3 to 4.4 g/kg/d in trained people show no additional lean mass over 1.8 to 2.5 g/kg/d, and also no fat gain despite the extra calories and no adverse markers over up to a year. The practical value of higher protein beyond muscle is appetite control and diet composition (higher protein share lowers spontaneous energy intake), which is established at moderate levels (about 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/d, 25 to 30 % of energy) and not shown to keep increasing at very high intakes.

### Cited Findings

- Antonio et al. 2014, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:19: 30 resistance-trained adults, 8 weeks, 4.4 ± 0.8 versus 1.8 ± 0.4 g/kg/d (307 versus 138 g/d, higher energy intake in the high group), unchanged training: no differences in body weight, fat mass or FFM. (verified, abstract): [Antonio 2014, PubMed 24834017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24834017/)
- Antonio et al. 2015, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 12:39: 48 trained adults, 8 weeks of periodised heavy RT, 3.4 versus 2.3 g/kg/d: FFM +1.5 kg in both groups; fat mass -1.7 versus -0.3 kg; body weight -0.1 versus +1.3 kg. Intake was self-reported and groups were unequal in size. (verified, abstract): [Antonio 2015, PubMed 26500462](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26500462/)
- Antonio et al. 2016, J Nutr Metab 2016:9104792: 14 resistance-trained men, one-year crossover, 3.32 ± 0.87 versus 2.51 ± 0.69 g/kg/d: no harmful effects on blood lipids, liver or kidney markers, no gain in fat mass. (verified, abstract): [Antonio 2016, PubMed 27807480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/)
- ISSN: intakes above 3.0 g/kg/d "may have positive effects on body composition in resistance-trained individuals (i.e., promote loss of fat mass)": [Jäger 2017, PubMed 28642676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- Leidy et al. 2015, Am J Clin Nutr 101(6):1320S-1329S, review: diets with 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/d and 25 to 30 g protein per meal improve appetite, body weight management and cardiometabolic risk factors; the acute satiety effect is modest; long-term results are limited by adherence. (verified, abstract): [Leidy 2015, PubMed 25926512](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/)
- Weigle et al. 2005, Am J Clin Nutr 82(1):41-48: 19 adults; raising protein from 15 % to 30 % of energy at constant carbohydrate, then eating ad libitum for 12 weeks: spontaneous intake fell by 441 ± 63 kcal/d, weight -4.9 ± 0.5 kg, fat mass -3.7 ± 0.4 kg. (verified, abstract): [Weigle 2005, PubMed 16002798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/)
- Protein leverage: Gosby et al. 2014, Obes Rev 15(3):183-191, analysis of 38 experimental ad libitum trials with protein from 8 to 54 % of energy: percent protein was negatively associated with total energy intake (F = 6.9, P < 0.0001), regardless of whether fat or carbohydrate diluted the protein. (verified, abstract): [Gosby 2014, PubMed 24588967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24588967/)
- Higher protein in dieters raised resting energy expenditure by 595.5 kJ/d (about 142 kcal/d, CI 67.0 to 1124.1 kJ) relative to standard protein: [Wycherley 2012, PubMed 23097268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23097268/)
- Cost of high protein: in Mettler 2010 the high-protein group (2.3 g/kg/d, 35 % of energy) reported more fatigue and a worse general state than the control group: [Mettler 2010, PubMed 19927027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19927027/). Refalo et al. point to the trade-off with carbohydrate and fat inside a fixed calorie budget: [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)

### Inferences

- For muscle, nothing shows a benefit above roughly 2.2 to 2.5 g/kg/d in energy balance or surplus. Presets above about 2.2 g/kg/d make sense only for the deficit case (section 4).
- Extra protein calories appear to be stored as fat less readily than predicted (Antonio 2014, 2015), but these are small, self-reported, partly non-blinded studies from one group; do not build calorie math on it.
- The satiety argument justifies a high protein share in a diet preset independently of the muscle argument; it is supported at 25 to 30 % of energy, not specifically at 2.5 g/kg and above.

### Gaps

- Quantitative thermic effect of protein (the usual 20 to 30 % figure) was not researched and has no source here.
- No trial compares satiety at 1.6 versus 2.4 g/kg/d directly.
- Antonio's studies have no independent replication at 3 g/kg and above that I found.

## 6. Safety: kidney, bone, upper limits, long-term data

### Takeaway

In healthy adults, RCT meta-analyses show no decline in kidney function and no bone harm from higher protein intakes; kidney filtration rises slightly within the normal range. No authority has set an upper limit; trial evidence covers up to about 2.5 to 3.3 g/kg/d for a year in small samples, long-term (multi-year) data at 2 g/kg and above are thin, and existing kidney disease is the clear exception.

### Cited Findings

- Devries et al. 2018, J Nutr 148(11):1760-1775, meta-analysis of 28 RCTs with 1358 participants without kidney disease; high protein defined as 1.5 g/kg/d or more, 20 % of energy or more, or 100 g/d or more. Post-intervention GFR was slightly higher with high protein (SMD 0.19, CI 0.07 to 0.31), the change in GFR did not differ (SMD 0.11, CI -0.05 to 0.27, p = 0.16). (verified, abstract): [Devries 2018, PubMed 30383278](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30383278/)
- Van Elswyk et al. 2018, Adv Nutr 9(4):404-418, systematic review of 26 studies (RCTs and observational) on intakes above the US RDA: 8 of 13 RCTs found a higher GFR within the normal range; evidence on kidney stones limited and inconsistent; no adverse effect on blood pressure; most trials shorter than 6 months, moderate to high risk of bias. (verified, abstract): [Van Elswyk 2018, PubMed 30032227](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30032227/)
- Counterview: Ko et al. 2020, J Am Soc Nephrol 31(8):1667-1679, review: high protein causes glomerular hyperfiltration and may over the long term contribute to new kidney disease; caution is advised for people at risk (largely observational and mechanistic evidence). (verified, abstract): [Ko 2020, PubMed 32669325](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32669325/)
- Bone: Shams-White et al. 2017, Am J Clin Nutr 105(6):1528-1543, National Osteoporosis Foundation review of 16 RCTs and 20 cohorts: higher protein had no adverse effect on bone; lumbar spine BMD +0.52 % (CI 0.06 to 0.97) in favour of higher protein. (verified, abstract): [Shams-White 2017, PubMed 28404575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28404575/)
- One-year data: 3.32 versus 2.51 g/kg/d in 14 trained men without harmful changes in lipids, liver or kidney markers: [Antonio 2016, PubMed 27807480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/)
- Upper limits proposed by individual authors: Wu 2016, Food Funct 7(3):1251-1265 (narrative review, single author): long-term 2 g/kg/d is safe for healthy adults, the tolerable upper limit is 3.5 g/kg/d for well-adapted subjects, chronic intake above 2 g/kg/d "may result in digestive, renal, and vascular abnormalities and should be avoided". (verified, abstract): [Wu 2016, PubMed 26797090](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26797090/). Bilsborough and Mann 2006, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 16(2):129-152: danger arises when protein exceeds about 35 % of energy (hyperaminoacidaemia, hyperammonaemia, nausea, "rabbit starvation"); suggested maximum about 25 % of energy at 2 to 2.5 g/kg/d (176 g for an 80 kg person on 12,000 kJ); theoretical maximum from urea synthesis capacity 285 to 365 g/d for 80 kg. (verified, abstract): [Bilsborough 2006, PubMed 16779921](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16779921/)
- Regulators: EFSA set no upper level and calls intakes up to twice the PRI (about 1.7 g/kg/d) safe as commonly consumed: [FoodNavigator on EFSA 2012](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2012/02/10/EFSA-sets-recommended-EU-protein-intakes-at-0.8g-kg-per-day). The US DRI report set no UL for protein because data were insufficient (via Wolfe et al. 2017): [Wolfe 2017, PMC5347101](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5347101)
- DGE sports working group: kidney function should be checked before a deliberately very high intake: [König 2020 PDF](https://www.ernaehrungs-umschau.de/fileadmin/Ernaehrungs-Umschau/pdfs/pdf_2020/07_20/EU07_2020_PR_DGE_Sport_eng.pdf) (search snippet). PROT-AGE excludes people with eGFR under 30 without dialysis from its higher recommendations: [Bauer 2013, PubMed 23867520](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/)
- ISSN 2024 review of protein misconceptions (Antonio et al., J Int Soc Sports Nutr 21(1):2341903) addresses kidney, bone and fat-gain concerns: [Antonio 2024, PubMed 38626029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38626029/) (the abstract lists the questions but not the answers)

### Inferences

- For healthy adults, presets up to 2.0 to 2.2 g/kg/d need no safety warning beyond "not for people with kidney disease, ask your doctor". A hard technical cap around 3.0 to 3.5 g/kg reference weight stays inside what Wu and Bilsborough consider tolerable and inside what trials have tested.
- Wu's two statements (2 g/kg safe long term, above 2 g/kg chronically to be avoided) are an opinion in a narrative review; they conflict with the one-year trial data and are not backed by a cited dose-finding study in the abstract.
- Conflict of interest context: several reassuring reviews come from authors with industry ties (see Gaps); the cautionary nephrology view relies on observational data. Neither side has multi-year RCTs.

### Gaps

- No RCT longer than about one to two years at 2 g/kg/d or more in healthy adults was found; long-term safety is extrapolated.
- Funding and affiliations of Devries 2018 and Van Elswyk 2018 (reported industry links) were not verified in the abstracts.
- Answers of Antonio 2024 to the individual questions were not read.
- Cardiovascular and cancer outcomes by protein source (animal versus plant) were outside what I researched.

## 7. Which reference weight: total body weight, FFM or adjusted weight? Sex, age, plant-based diets

### Takeaway

Protein needs track lean mass, not fat mass: per kg total weight works for normal-weight people, but in overweight and obesity it overshoots, so sources use either FFM (about 1.9 to 2.5 g/kg FFM), a reference or ideal weight, or a reduced per-kg figure (1.2 to 1.5 g/kg actual weight). Older adults need more per meal and at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/d; evidence in women is thinner and points to similar or slightly lower needs per kg; at matched high intakes (1.6 to 2.0 g/kg/d) well-planned vegan diets build muscle equally well, and no primary source for a fixed "+10 to 20 %" plant surcharge was found.

### Cited Findings

- FFM as the better scaler: in Refalo 2025 the relation between protein and FFM retention was more certain per kg FFM (99 % probability positive) than per kg body mass (97 %), and recommendations are given in both units (1.9 g/kg body mass = 2.5 g/kg FFM): [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)
- Helms: recommendations expressed per kg lean mass, 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg: [Helms 2014a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/), [Helms 2014b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)
- Stronger by Science (secondary): about 2.0 g/kg body mass for men (2.35 to be safe), about 1.75 for women (2.05 to be safe), or 2.35 g/kg FFM (2.75 to be safe) for both: [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/protein-science/)
- Obesity: Weijs and Wolfe 2016, Clin Nutr 35(2):394-398: in 60 obese older adults during weight loss, the intake cut-off associated with muscle preservation was 1.2 g/kg body weight or 1.9 g/kg FFM (odds ratios 5.4 and 8.1). (verified, abstract): [Weijs 2016, PubMed 25788405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25788405/)
- Overweight and obesity during weight loss: above 1.3 g/kg/d (actual weight) muscle mass is preserved or increased, below 1.0 g/kg/d the risk of loss rises: [Kokura 2024, PubMed 39002131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39002131/). Examine (via mirror): 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/d if overweight: [Examine guide](https://examine.com/guides/protein-intake/)
- Per meal by lean mass and age: plateau at 0.25 g/kg lean mass in young and 0.60 g/kg lean mass in older men: [Moore 2015, PubMed 25056502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)
- Age: reference values 1.0 g/kg/d from 65 ([Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30904906/)), 1.0 to 1.2 and 1.2 or more when active ([Bauer 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/), [Deutz 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814383/)); in Nunes 2022 adults of 65 and over benefited at 1.2 to 1.59 g/kg/d with RT: [Nunes 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/). In Morton 2018 the extra FFM from supplementation fell with age: [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- Sex: IAAO breakpoint in trained women 1.49 to 1.53 g/kg/d versus 1.7 to 2.0 in trained men: [Malowany 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30395050/), [Bandegan 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28179492/), [Mazzulla 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31618421/). In Refalo 2025 the slope was 0.09 in men and 0.05 in women with wide intervals: [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)
- Plant versus animal: Hevia-Larraín et al. 2021, Sports Med 51(6):1317-1330: 19 vegans versus 19 omnivores, both at 1.6 g/kg/d (soy versus whey supplement), 12 weeks RT: no differences in muscle mass or strength gains: [Hevia-Larraín 2021, PubMed 33599941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33599941/). Monteyne et al. 2023, J Nutr 153(6):1680-1695: vegan versus omnivorous high-protein diet (about 2 g/kg/d), 10 weeks RT, lean mass +3.1 versus +2.6 kg, no difference: [Monteyne 2023, PubMed 36822394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36822394/). Messina et al. 2018, meta-analysis of 9 studies (266 participants): soy and animal protein give the same strength and lean mass gains with RT: [Messina 2018, PubMed 29722584](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722584/). Lim et al. 2021, Nutrients 13(2):661, 16 RCTs: no difference in absolute lean mass overall, but animal protein favoured in adults under 50 (+0.41 kg, CI 0.08 to 0.74): [Lim 2021, PubMed 33670701](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33670701/)
- Mechanism for a plant surcharge: Pinckaers et al. 2021, Sports Med 51(Suppl 1):59-74: plant proteins (soy, wheat) give lower post-meal muscle protein synthesis per gram because of digestibility and lower essential amino acid, lysine and methionine content; this "may be compensated for by consuming a greater amount", blends or fortification; whether plant-based diets raise the overall requirement still needs clinical studies. (verified, abstract): [Pinckaers 2021, PubMed 34515966](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34515966/)

### Inferences

- For the app, total body weight is fine up to about BMI 25 to 27. Above that, compute from a reference weight. Options in order of evidence fit: (a) if body fat is known: 2.0 to 2.5 g/kg FFM; (b) reference weight = weight at BMI 25 for the person's height (my proposal for a simple rule; equivalent to the "ideal or desirable body weight" used clinically); (c) 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg actual weight. Example: 120 kg, 1.80 m: BMI 25 weight = 81 kg; 1.6 to 2.0 g/kg of that = 130 to 162 g = 1.1 to 1.35 g/kg actual weight, consistent with (c) and with Weijs' 1.2 g/kg cut-off.
- Conversion for lean users: body mass figure = FFM figure × (1 - body fat fraction). At 15 % fat, 2.35 g/kg FFM = 2.0 g/kg body mass; at 25 % fat it is 1.76 g/kg body mass. That alone explains most of the suggested sex difference (women carry more fat per kg), so a separate sex factor is unnecessary when a reference weight or FFM is used; without body fat data, about 10 % less per kg for women is a low-certainty option.
- Age 65 and over: floor of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/d instead of 0.8, and the training presets stay the same; older users benefit from protein at lower thresholds but not less.
- Plant-based: at 1.6 g/kg/d and above, outcome trials show no disadvantage, so a surcharge matters mainly at low intakes (near 0.8 to 1.2 g/kg/d). An optional +10 to 20 % for fully plant-based users is a reasonable safety margin from the mechanism, but it is an assumption, not a cited finding.

### Gaps

- No primary source for "+10 to 20 % protein for vegans or vegetarians" was found; the figure circulates in secondary literature.
- The specific rules attributed to Helms (g per cm of height) and Kevin Hall (goal weight) were not found.
- Clinical guideline wording on adjusted body weight in obesity (for example ESPEN, ASPEN) was only seen in secondary summaries and is not cited here.
- Possible soy industry ties of Messina were not verified.
- No dose-response study in women above about 1.5 g/kg/d.

## 8. Is the cap at 35 % of energy (AMDR upper bound) justified, and what happens in aggressive deficits?

### Takeaway

The 35 % upper bound of the AMDR is not a toxicity threshold for a g/kg target: the DRI report set no upper limit for protein, and for reference adults in energy balance 35 % corresponds to about 3.7 g/kg/d. In a deficit, a percent cap cuts protein exactly when the evidence says needs rise (the successful high-protein arm in Mettler 2010 sat at 35 % of energy at only 2.3 g/kg/d), so an absolute cap in g/kg of reference weight is better supported than a percent cap.

### Cited Findings

- Wolfe et al. 2017, Adv Nutr 8(2):266-275 ("Optimizing protein intake in adults: interpretation and application of the RDA compared with the AMDR"): the AMDR of 10 to 35 % of energy expresses protein in the context of a complete diet and equals 1.05 to 3.67 g/kg/d for reference adults (57 and 70 kg) at their energy requirement; the Food and Nutrition Board notes possible benefits above the RDA and set no UL for protein. (secondary reading via search summary of the paper): [Wolfe 2017, PMC5347101](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5347101)
- The DRI report found data insufficient to set a tolerable upper intake level for protein or amino acids: [National Academies, DRI chapter](https://nap.edu/read/11537/chapter/14)
- A 2024 National Academies letter report on rethinking the AMDR repeats the criticism that the percent range does not line up with the g/kg-based RDA (10 % of energy is more than 0.8 g/kg/d for a person in energy balance): [National Academies 2024, Rethinking the AMDR](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27957/chapter/6)
- Teaching text summarising the IOM rationale: the AMDR upper end for protein was set to complement the fat and carbohydrate ranges, not from evidence of harm (secondary): [Open Oregon, Nutrition Science, protein chapter](https://openoregon.pressbooks.pub/nutritionscience2e/chapter/6e-consequences-too-little-much-protein/)
- A deficit trial at the cap: 2.3 g/kg/d was already 35 % of energy at 60 % of habitual intake and protected lean mass (-0.3 versus -1.6 kg): [Mettler 2010, PubMed 19927027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19927027/)
- Recommendations for dieting athletes exceed what a 35 % cap allows at low calories: 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg/d ([Hector 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182451/)), 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg lean mass ([Helms 2014b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)), 1.9 to 3.2 g/kg ([Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content))
- The argument for a ceiling: Bilsborough and Mann 2006 locate the danger where protein exceeds about 35 % of energy and suggest about 25 % of energy (2 to 2.5 g/kg/d) as a prudent maximum, but derive the hard physiological limit in grams per day (285 to 365 g/d for 80 kg, from urea synthesis capacity), not in percent: [Bilsborough 2006, PubMed 16779921](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16779921/)
- Experimental diets with protein up to 54 % of energy have been used in ad libitum trials (protein leverage dataset): [Gosby 2014, PubMed 24588967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24588967/)
- Helms recommends 15 to 30 % of energy from fat during contest preparation, which sets the competing floor inside a small calorie budget: [Helms 2014b, PubMed 24864135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)

### Inferences

- Worked example (my calculation): 80 kg person, target 2.0 g/kg = 160 g = 640 kcal. At 2500 kcal that is 26 % of energy (cap inactive). At 1800 kcal it is 36 % (cap reduces protein to 157 g). At 1500 kcal it is 43 %: the cap cuts protein to 131 g = 1.64 g/kg, a level at which the deficit trials and Refalo's trend line (zero loss at about 1.9 g/kg) predict more lean mass loss. The cap therefore works against the purpose of the "diet / muscle retention" preset.
- The "rabbit starvation" concern is about diets that are almost only protein in absolute amounts near the urea synthesis limit (about 3.5 to 4.5 g/kg/d), not about 40 to 45 % protein in a 1500 kcal diet that still contains 130 to 160 g of protein. A percent rule is a poor proxy for that risk.
- Better-supported guard rails for the calculation: (1) cap protein in absolute terms at about 2.5 g/kg reference weight for presets (hard maximum about 3.0 to 3.5 g/kg); (2) keep a fat minimum (for example 0.5 g/kg, or 15 to 20 % of energy) and let carbohydrate take what is left; (3) if protein plus fat minimum exceed the calorie target, the deficit is too aggressive and the app should say so instead of silently lowering protein.
- If a percent cap is kept for sanity, it should apply only at or above maintenance calories, or be raised to about 45 % in a deficit.

### Gaps

- The primary IOM 2005 text on how the 35 % bound was derived was not read; the "complement to fat and carbohydrate" rationale comes from a teaching text.
- Wolfe 2017 was not read in full; the 1.05 to 3.67 g/kg conversion comes from a search summary of the paper.
- No trial tests long-term diets above 35 % protein of energy in free-living people for safety end points.

## Synthesis: numbers a calculation could use (inference, built on the sections above)

### Takeaway

Keep 1.6 g/kg/d as the default, treat 2.0 to 2.2 as a legitimate "maximise" option and raise the deficit preset to about 2.0 to 2.4, computed on a reference weight for users with overweight, with an absolute instead of a percent cap.

### Cited Findings

- Minimum for sedentary adults 0.8 g/kg/d (0.83 EFSA), 1.0 from 65: [Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30904906/); IAAO suggests 0.93 average and 1.2 safe: [Humayun 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17921376/)
- Training adults 1.2 to 2.0 or 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/d: [Jäger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/); muscle gain 1.6 to 2.2: [Iraki 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/), [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- Deficit 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg/d: [Hector 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182451/); zero-loss trend at 1.9 g/kg body mass or 2.5 g/kg FFM, lean and aggressive up to 3.2: [Refalo 2025 PDF](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/3480bb69-c09c-4e5c-814e-5c232ea07e27/content)
- Overweight: 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg actual weight or 1.9 g/kg FFM: [Weijs 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25788405/), [Kokura 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39002131/)

### Inferences

- Proposed table (g/kg reference weight per day; reference weight = actual weight up to BMI 25, above that the weight at BMI 25, or FFM-based when body fat is known):
  - Sedentary, no training, energy balance: 1.0 (range 0.8 to 1.2); from 65 years: 1.2 (1.0 to 1.2).
  - Active, general default: 1.6 (range 1.2 to 2.0).
  - Muscle gain with RT (balance or surplus): 1.8 (range 1.6 to 2.2); "maximise" option 2.2.
  - Fat loss with RT, moderate deficit: 2.0 (range 1.8 to 2.4).
  - Fat loss, lean user (men under about 12 to 15 %, women under about 20 to 23 % fat) or deficit above about 25 %: 2.3 (range 2.0 to 2.6), equal to about 2.5 to 3.1 g/kg FFM.
  - Fat loss without training, overweight: 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg actual weight (equals about 1.6 to 2.0 on the BMI 25 weight).
  - Cap: 2.5 g/kg reference weight for presets, absolute maximum 3.0 to 3.5; no 35 % of energy cap in a deficit; fat floor 0.5 g/kg or 15 to 20 % of energy.
- Wording for the presets: 1.6 "covers most of the benefit", 2.0 to 2.2 "small possible extra, useful when dieting or for maximum gains". Avoid both "more is useless" and "more is clearly better".
- Certainty: high that 1.2 to 1.6 beats 0.8 with training; moderate that deficits raise needs; low for any benefit above 1.6 in energy balance (plausible, small); low for sex and plant adjustments.

### Gaps

- The BMI 25 reference weight rule and the body fat thresholds for "lean" are my operationalisation; the thresholds follow Refalo's "under about 12 % men, 20 % women" loosely and are not validated cut-offs.
- No source defines the deficit size at which the higher preset should start; "above about 25 %" is a judgment based on the 40 % deficits in Longland, Mettler and Pasiakos and the 300 kcal subgroup split in Refalo.
