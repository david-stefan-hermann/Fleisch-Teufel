# Energy expenditure of healthy adults: evidence for the calculation in a nutrition tracker (state: November 2026)

Reading notes: "verified" means read in the abstract or full text (Europe PMC / NCBI efetch / fetched page). "Snippet" means seen only in a search result excerpt and not checked in the primary text. kcal conversions of MJ values are my own arithmetic (1 MJ = 239 kcal). Evidence labels: [consensus], [contested], [single study].

## How accurate are BMR/RMR prediction equations against indirect calorimetry, and which is best for which group?

### Takeaway

Mifflin-St Jeor is the best supported default for normal weight and overweight adults: unbiased on group level and within ±10 % of measured RMR in roughly 70 to 80 % of people, but individual errors of 20 % or more remain and no weight/height/age equation removes them. For trained, muscular people Mifflin underestimates (accuracy below 50 % in recreational athletes); FFM-based equations (Cunningham) or the athlete equation of ten Haaf and Weijs perform clearly better there.

### Cited Findings

General population, normal weight and mixed

- Mifflin, St Jeor et al. 1990 (Am J Clin Nutr 51:241-247), n = 498 healthy adults (247 women, 251 men), 19 to 78 y, 264 normal weight and 234 obese: REE = 9.99 x weight(kg) + 6.25 x height(cm) - 4.92 x age + 166 x sex(M=1) - 161, R² = 0.71; simplified: men 10W + 6.25H - 5A + 5, women 10W + 6.25H - 5A - 161. In this sample Harris-Benedict overestimated measured REE by 5 %. FFM was the best single predictor: REE = 19.7 x FFM + 413 (R² = 0.64). Verified. [Mifflin 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/)
- Frankenfield, Roth-Yousey, Compher 2005 (J Am Diet Assoc 105:775-789), systematic review comparing Harris-Benedict, Mifflin-St Jeor, Owen and WHO/FAO/UNU: Mifflin-St Jeor "was the most reliable, predicting RMR within 10% of measured in more nonobese and obese individuals than any other equation, and it also had the narrowest error range"; noteworthy errors exist for individuals; older adults and ethnic minorities are underrepresented in validation work. Verified (abstract). [consensus] [Frankenfield 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/)
- Frankenfield et al. 2003: share of individuals more than 10 % off was 22 % with Mifflin, 33 % with Harris-Benedict and 35 % with Owen. Snippet only. [Frankenfield 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12963943/)
- Roza and Shizgal 1984 (Am J Clin Nutr 40:168-182) re-evaluated the Harris-Benedict data (239 subjects) plus 98 additional subjects; the precision of the revised equation is stated as about ±14 %. Verified (abstract). [Roza and Shizgal 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6741850/)
- Henry 2005 (Public Health Nutr 8:1133-1152): Oxford equations built from 10 552 BMR values. The Schofield database (7173 values) contains 3388 Italians (47 %), whose BMR per kg is high; Schofield/FAO equations therefore overestimate BMR in many populations. The Oxford equations give lower BMR values than Schofield for men 18 to 60 y and for all women above 18 y. Verified (abstract). [Henry 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16277825/)
- Müller et al. 2004 (Am J Clin Nutr 80:1379-1390): German reference database, n = 2528, age 5 to 91 y, 7 centers, 1985 to 2002. WHO equations overestimated REE at low REE and underestimated at high REE; the new regressions explain up to 75 % of the variance; BMI-group specific equations were derived; normative data of the IOM were underestimated by 0.3 MJ/d (about 72 kcal). Verified (abstract). [Müller 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15531690/)
- Biological ceiling of any equation: Johnstone et al. 2005 (Am J Clin Nutr 82:941-948), n = 150: FFM explains 63 % of the variance in BMR, fat mass 6 %, age 2 %; 26 % remains unexplained; within-subject variation is 2 %; sex was not a significant predictor once body composition was included. Verified. [Johnstone 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16280423/)
- Pavlidou et al. 2023 (Metabolites 13:189), n = 722 Caucasian adults: new equations men 9.65W + 573H(m) - 5.08A + 260, women 7.38W + 607H(m) - 2.31A + 43. Verified (abstract). [single study] [Pavlidou 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36837808/)

Overweight and obesity

- Madden, Mulrooney, Shah 2016 (J Hum Nutr Diet 29:458-476), systematic review for BMI ≥ 25 (21 studies on REE, 4 on TEE): the most accurate equation differs by BMI class (WHO weight+height equations at BMI ≥ 25 and ≥ 30, Mifflin at BMI 30 to 39.9, Henry at BMI ≥ 40); Mifflin was the most precise, with about 75 % of predictions within 10 %, so errors exceed 10 % in about 25 % of people; there is no accurate prediction equation for total energy expenditure in obesity. Verified (abstract). [Madden 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26923904/)
- Weijs 2008 (Am J Clin Nutr 88:959-970), BMI 25 to 40: in US adults (180 women, 158 men) Mifflin was accurate (within 10 %) in 79 %, bias -1.0 %, RMSE 136 kcal/d. In Dutch adults no equation was accurate: FAO/WHO/UNU weight equation 68 % accurate with RMSE 178 kcal/d in overweight, Lazzer equation 69 % with RMSE 215 kcal/d in obese. Verified (abstract). [Weijs 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18842782/)
- Systematic review and meta-analysis 2022 (Nutr Rev 80:2113-2135; first author Macena, author list not re-verified), 61 studies in overweight and obesity: lowest bias for the FAO/WHO/UNU 1985 weight equation in overweight (mean difference 8.97 kcal, 95 % CI -26.99 to 44.94) and Lazzer 2007 in obesity (4.70 kcal); highest precision (share within 10 %) only 60.65 % (overweight) and 62.54 % (obesity) for Harris-Benedict 1919; equations based on body composition had the highest bias in this population; heterogeneity I² > 90 %. Verified (abstract). [contested which equation is best] [PMID 35551409](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35551409/)

Older adults

- Cioffi et al. 2021 (Clin Nutr 40:3094-3103), 14 studies in older adults: least bias for Mifflin (-0.3 %) and Harris-Benedict (+2.6 %); bias above 5 % for FAO/WHO/UNU, Fredrix and Müller; share within 10 % about 70 % for Harris-Benedict, 65 % for Henry and 61 % for Mifflin. Verified (abstract). [Cioffi 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33288302/)
- Porter et al. 2023 (Am J Clin Nutr 117:1164-1173), n = 1686 aged ≥ 65 y: RMR (kJ/d) = 31.524 x W + 25.851 x H(cm) - 24.432 x age + 486.268 x sex(M=1) + 530.557; group bias about 1 %, individual limits of agreement about ±25 %. Verified (abstract). [Porter 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37054885/)
- Pontzer et al. 2021 (Science 373:808-812), n = 6421 doubly labeled water measurements: size-adjusted total and basal expenditure are stable in adulthood; segmented regression puts the break point of adjusted TEE at 63.0 y (95 % CI 60.1 to 65.9) and of adjusted basal expenditure at 46.5 y (95 % CI 40.6 to 52.4, imprecise); after about 60 y adjusted TEE declines by 0.7 ± 0.1 % per year; people in their nineties are about 26 % below middle-aged adults. Verified (full text). [Pontzer 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34385400/)

Muscular and athletic people

- Cunningham 1980 (Am J Clin Nutr 33:2372-2374), 223 subjects of the Harris-Benedict data: BMR = 500 + 22 x LBM(kg). Verified. [Cunningham 1980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7435418/)
- Cunningham 1991 (Am J Clin Nutr 54:963-969): REE = 370 + 21.6 x FFM(kg); FFM explains 65 to 90 % of the variation in REE across studies. Verified (abstract). [Cunningham 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1957828/)
- ten Haaf and Weijs 2014 (PLoS One 9:e108460), 90 recreational athletes (53 men, 37 women), 18 to 35 y, about 9 h sport per week: Harris-Benedict, WHO, Schofield, Mifflin and Owen were each accurate (within 10 %) in fewer than 50 % of athletes; new equations: REE (kJ/d) = 49.940 x W + 2459.053 x H(m) - 34.014 x age + 799.257 x sex(M=1) + 122.502, or REE (kJ/d) = 95.272 x FFM + 2026.161; the Cunningham equation performed about as well. Verified (abstract). [ten Haaf and Weijs 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275434/)
- Jagim et al. 2018 (J Strength Cond Res 32:1875-1881), 22 female and 28 male athletes: all five tested equations underestimated RMR; Cunningham had the smallest mean difference (-165 kcal); best RMSE in men Harris-Benedict (284 kcal), in women Cunningham (110 kcal). Verified (abstract). [Jagim 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28682934/)
- Tinsley, Graybeal, Moore 2019 (Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 44:397-406), 27 physique athletes (17 men, 10 women): most existing equations underestimated; new equations RMR = 24.8 x body weight + 10 and RMR = 25.9 x FFM + 284; ten Haaf and Cunningham 1980 were acceptable; validated only by leave-one-out. Verified (abstract). [single study, small] [Tinsley 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30240568/)
- O'Neill, Corish, Horner 2023 (Sports Med 53:2373-2398), systematic review with meta-analysis, 29 studies, 1430 athletes (822 women, 608 men), 100 equations: not significantly different from measured RMR were Cunningham 1980, Harris-Benedict 1918, Cunningham 1991, De Lorenzo and ten Haaf; ten Haaf was the most precise with 80.2 % within ±10 %, the others 40.7 to 63.7 %. Verified (abstract). [O'Neill 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37632665/)

### Inferences

- Concrete error budget for the app: with Mifflin expect a group bias near 0, an RMSE of roughly 135 to 215 kcal/d and about one in four to five users more than 10 % off (for an RMR of 1700 kcal that is more than ±170 kcal before any activity factor is applied). Because the activity factor multiplies this error, a 10 % RMR error becomes 200 to 300 kcal/d of TDEE error.
- Keeping Mifflin as the default is in line with the reviews for normal weight, overweight and older users. The Harris-Benedict variants are not better overall (+5 % bias in Mifflin's sample, better precision only in some obesity and older-adult analyses).
- For users who state a body fat percentage and train regularly, an FFM-based option is justified: Cunningham 1980 (500 + 22 x FFM) or ten Haaf FFM (22.8 x FFM + 484 in kcal, my conversion of 95.272 and 2026.161 kJ). The "Katch-McArdle" formula circulating in fitness calculators (370 + 21.6 x LBM) is numerically the Cunningham 1991 equation. Caveat: in overweight and obese groups body composition equations had the highest bias (2022 meta-analysis), and a self-estimated body fat percentage adds its own error, so this should be an opt-in for lean, trained users, not a general replacement.
- Without body fat data, the ten Haaf weight/height/age equation is the best validated choice for recreational athletes (in kcal: 11.94 x W + 587.7 x H(m) - 8.13 x age + 191.0 x sex(M=1) + 29.3, my conversion). A plausible app rule: offer it when the user reports several hours of training per week.
- Sex differences in RMR are largely explained by fat-free mass (Johnstone), which is why the sex constant of Mifflin (166 kcal) fits average body composition and misfits very lean women or men with high body fat.
- Since 26 % of BMR variance is unexplained by body composition and age, an own-data correction (see the section on adaptive TDEE) is the only way to remove the individual error.

### Gaps

- Exact per-equation percentages within ±10 % from the Frankenfield 2005 review (the often quoted 82 % nonobese and 70 % obese for Mifflin) could not be verified in the full text; only the abstract statement is cited.
- No primary validation paper for "Katch-McArdle" was found; its attribution to the textbook of McArdle, Katch and Katch is from memory.
- The exact coefficients of the Müller 2004 equations were not verified and are not given here.
- Guessed DOIs for Frankenfield 2013 (obese vs nonobese validation) and Hasson 2011 resolved to wrong or missing papers; those studies are not cited.
- A 2026 systematic review protocol on RMR equations in athletes exists (Navalta, JMIR Res Protoc, DOI 10.2196/82482, snippet only); results were not available. Fields et al. 2022 (collegiate athletes, Tinsley and Freire equations with the lowest RMSE in men, about 404 and 412 kcal) was seen as snippet only.
- Evidence specifically for recreational strength trainees (as opposed to competitive athletes or physique athletes) is thin; the Tinsley sample has 27 people.
- Women-specific accuracy (cycle phase, hormonal contraception, menopause) was not researched in depth.

## How valid are activity multipliers (PAL 1.2 to 1.9), what does doubly labeled water show, and how accurate are MET-based and step-based calories on top of them?

### Takeaway

The PAL concept (TEE = BMR x PAL, range about 1.4 to 2.4 for sustainable lifestyles) is well founded in doubly labeled water data, but the popular five-step scale 1.2/1.375/1.55/1.725/1.9 has no traceable validation, self-chosen categories are unreliable, and even the best population equation predicts individual TEE only within about -25 % to +34 %. Adding logged exercise at gross MET values on top of a PAL counts the resting share twice, and observational DLW data suggest that on average only about 72 % of activity calories show up in total expenditure (contested).

### Cited Findings

PAL categories of the authorities

- FAO/WHO/UNU 2001 (report published 2004), Table 5.3: sedentary or light activity lifestyle PAL 1.40 to 1.69, active or moderately active 1.70 to 1.99, vigorous or vigorously active 2.00 to 2.40; "PAL values > 2.40 are difficult to maintain over a long period of time"; a PAL of 1.21 was judged too low even for emergency relief (1.40 suggested); one hour of moderate to vigorous exercise per day can raise an average PAL from 1.55 to 1.75; BMR from the Schofield equations (for example men 18 to 30: 0.063 x kg + 2.896 MJ/d, women 18 to 30: 0.062 x kg + 2.036 MJ/d); requirement = PAL x BMR by the factorial method. Verified (fetched page). [consensus] [FAO/WHO/UNU 2001, chapter 5](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e07.htm)
- EFSA 2013 (EFSA Journal 11(1):3005): average requirement = REE from the Henry 2005 equations x PAL 1.4 (low active), 1.6 (moderately active), 1.8 (active), 2.0 (very active); sustainable PAL values observed in free-living people range from about 1.35 to 2.5. Verified (EFSA page/abstract). [EFSA 2013](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/3005)
- NASEM 2023 Dietary Reference Intakes for Energy: four categories derived from approximate quartiles of PAL in DLW studies: inactive 1.0 ≤ PAL < 1.53, low active 1.53 to < 1.68, active 1.68 to < 1.85, very active 1.85 to < 2.50; separate TEE prediction equations per category (inputs sex, age, height, weight). Snippet level (National Academies Press chapter excerpts), equations and their standard errors not read. [NASEM 2023, chapter 5](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/26818/chapter/5)
- Origin of 1.2/1.375/1.55/1.725/1.9: a targeted search found the values only on calculator and popular sites, without a primary source; the historical review of the Harris-Benedict studies (Frankenfield, Muth, Rowe 1998, J Am Diet Assoc) concerns the BMR equations, not activity factors. [Frankenfield 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9550168/)

Doubly labeled water data

- Black et al. 1996 (Eur J Clin Nutr 50:72-92), 574 DLW measurements: the range of sustainable PAL runs from about 1.2 (chair-bound or bed-bound) to about 4.5 (elite endurance events, not sustainable); after adjustment women expend about 11 % less than men; PAL values of active free-living people were higher than previously assumed. Verified (abstract). [Black 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8641250/)
- Westerterp 2018 (Proc Nutr Soc 77:4-10): the maximum sustainable PAL for a vigorously active lifestyle is about 2.00 to 2.40; an exercise-induced increase in activity energy expenditure can be compensated by lower resting expenditure and lower non-exercise activity, especially in negative energy balance, and training improves efficiency in untrained people. Verified (abstract). [Westerterp 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28724452/)
- Thurber et al. 2019 (Sci Adv 5:eaaw0341): the long-term ceiling of sustained expenditure is about 2.5 x BMR. Verified (abstract). [Thurber 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31183404/)
- Pontzer et al. 2021 (Science): BMR accounts for roughly 50 to 70 % of TEE; size-adjusted TEE is stable from about 20 to 60 y. Verified. [Pontzer 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34385400/)
- Bajunaid et al. 2025 (Nat Food 6:58-71), IAEA DLW database, 6497 measurements, age 4 to 96 y (5899 for derivation, 598 for validation). Predictive equation without any activity input: ln(TEE MJ/d) = -0.2127 + 0.4167 x ln(weight kg) + 0.006565 x height(cm) - 0.02054 x age + 0.0003308 x age² - 0.000001852 x age³ + 0.09126 x ln(elevation m) - 0.04092 x sex (-1 male, +1 female) + ethnicity terms + interaction terms with elevation (- 0.0006759 x height x ln(elev) + 0.002018 x age x ln(elev) - 0.00002262 x age² x ln(elev) - 0.006947 x sex x ln(elev)). R² = 69.8 % of ln(TEE). 95 % prediction interval for an individual: lower = predicted x 0.7466 - 1.5405 MJ/d, upper = predicted x 1.3395 + 2.7668 MJ/d. 94.6 % of the validation sample fell inside. The equation underestimates athletes (n = 246) and pregnant or lactating women and "cannot be used for these populations". Analytical error of DLW about 7 %. A correction was published in April 2025 (content not checked). Verified (full text). [Bajunaid 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39806218/)
- McGrosky et al. 2025 (PNAS 122:e2420902122), 4213 adults from 34 populations: size-adjusted TEE and BEE are 6 to 11 % lower in economically more developed populations; energy intake plays a far greater role than expenditure for obesity. Verified (abstract). [McGrosky 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40658837/)

Self-report

- Lichtman et al. 1992 (NEJM 327:1893-1898): "diet-resistant" obese subjects under-reported intake by 47 ± 16 % and over-reported physical activity by 51 ± 75 % against DLW and measured body composition. Verified (abstract). [Lichtman 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/)
- Dhurandhar et al. 2015 (Int J Obes 39:1109-1113), expert statement: self-reported energy intake and physical activity energy expenditure are so inaccurate that they are "wholly unacceptable for scientific research" on energy balance. [Dhurandhar 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25394308/)

Additive vs constrained model, compensation

- Pontzer et al. 2016 (Curr Biol 26:410-417), n = 332 adults from five populations: TEE rises with accelerometer activity only in the lower range and plateaus in the upper deciles of activity (change of slope around 230 counts per minute per day). Verified (full text). [contested] [Pontzer 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26832439/)
- Pontzer et al. 2012 (PLoS One 7:e40503): TEE of Hadza hunter-gatherers does not differ from Western adults after controlling for body size. Verified (abstract). [Pontzer 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22848382/)
- Careau et al. 2021 (Curr Biol 31:4659-4666), n = 1754 adults from the IAEA DLW database: energy compensation averages 28 % through lower basal expenditure, so only about 72 % of extra activity calories translate into extra TEE; compensation is 29.7 % at the 10th BMI percentile and 45.7 % at the 90th. Cross-sectional; causality is open. Verified (full text). [contested] [Careau 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34453886/)
- Contradicting: Howard et al. 2025 (PNAS 122:e2519626122), observational: linear relation between physical activity and TEE (TEE = 0.00685 x PA + 7.124, R² = 0.35); the authors state that their data "do not support the constrained/compensated model". Verified (abstract). [contested] [Howard 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41118225/)
- Martin et al. 2019, E-MECHANIC trial (Am J Clin Nutr 110:583-592), n = 198 (171 per protocol), 24 weeks of supervised exercise at 8 or 20 kcal/kg/week: weight loss fell short of prediction by 1.5 kg and 2.7 kg; energy intake rose by 90.7 and 123.6 kcal/d; RMR and non-exercise activity did not change, so compensation happened through eating. Verified (abstract). [Martin 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31172175/)
- Riou et al. 2015 (Nutrients 7:3677-3704), 61 exercise intervention studies, 928 subjects: mean energy compensation 18 % ± 93 %, increasing with duration to about 84 % at around 80 weeks. Verified (abstract). [Riou 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25988763/)

MET values, gross vs net, steps, wearables

- Byrne et al. 2005 (J Appl Physiol 99:1112-1119), n = 769: measured resting values average 2.6 ± 0.4 ml O2/kg/min and 0.84 ± 0.16 kcal/kg/h; the conventional 1 MET (3.5 ml O2/kg/min, 1 kcal/kg/h) overestimates resting VO2 by 35 % and resting energy expenditure by 20 % on average; body composition (fat mass) explains most of the deviation. Verified (abstract). [Byrne 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15831804/)
- Herrmann et al. 2024, Adult Compendium of Physical Activities (J Sport Health Sci 13:6-12): 1114 activities, 912 with measured MET values, for adults 19 to 59 y; MET values are multiples of rest and include the resting share. Verified (abstract). [Herrmann 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38242596/); tables at [pacompendium.com](https://pacompendium.com)
- Hall C et al. 2004 (Med Sci Sports Exerc 36:2128-2134), n = 24: gross energy cost of 1600 m is 481 ± 20 kJ running and 340 ± 14 kJ walking (about 115 and 81 kcal per 1.6 km). Verified (abstract). [Hall 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15570150/)
- O'Driscoll et al. 2020 (Br J Sports Med 54:332-340), meta-analysis of 60 studies on wrist and arm wearables: accuracy of energy expenditure varies strongly by device and activity type, heterogeneity I² > 75 %. Verified (abstract). [O'Driscoll 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30194221/)
- Fuller et al. 2020 (JMIR mHealth uHealth 8:e18694), systematic review of 158 publications: step counts are accurate in laboratory settings; "For energy expenditure, no brand was accurate." Verified (abstract). [Fuller 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32897239/)

### Inferences

- The app's factor 1.2 for "no workouts" is below what FAO regards as compatible with a normal free-living life (1.40 to 1.69 for sedentary/light, 1.21 "too low") and below the NASEM inactive quartile (up to 1.53). A desk worker without sport realistically sits at about 1.4 to 1.5. If the scale 1.2 to 1.9 is meant as "everyday activity without training", the lower end is probably too low and the upper end (1.725, 1.9) too high for almost everyone who does not have a physical job: values above 1.9 without sport are rare.
- A cleaner, authority-backed design: non-exercise PAL in three or four steps (about 1.4 sitting, 1.5 to 1.6 sitting with some walking or standing, 1.7 to 1.8 mostly on the feet, 1.9 to 2.0 heavy physical work) and exercise added separately. EFSA and DGE use exactly the 1.4/1.6/1.8(/2.0) steps.
- Double counting: PAL x BMR already pays the resting cost of every minute of the day. A logged training must therefore be credited at net cost: kcal = (MET - 1) x kg x hours, or more exactly (MET - PAL_base) x BMR/24 x hours. Crediting gross MET x kg x h overstates a 60 minute session at 5 MET for a 75 kg person by about 75 to 100 kcal (20 to 25 %). On top of that, 1 kcal/kg/h overestimates real resting expenditure by about 20 % (Byrne), so "corrected METs" (MET x 3.5 / individual resting VO2, or simply kcal = MET x RMR_per_hour) would be more consistent with the app's own Mifflin value than MET x kg.
- Steps: the same logic. Steps are already partly contained in a non-exercise PAL (a PAL of 1.4 to 1.5 corresponds to a few thousand steps a day). Crediting all steps gross on top of the PAL counts twice; crediting only steps above a baseline, at net cost, avoids that. From Hall C 2004 the gross walking cost is about 81 kcal per 1.6 km for the study sample; the net cost is lower by the resting share of the walking time (my estimate: roughly 0.5 kcal/kg/km net vs about 0.75 gross; see Gaps).
- Compensation: if Careau's average holds, only about 70 % of credited activity calories are "really" available as extra food; in long interventions the effect is larger (Riou). This is contested (Howard 2025) and partly a behavioural effect via eating (E-MECHANIC), which a tracker already captures through logged intake. A defensible product decision is a user-visible option "credit trainings at 100 % / 50 % / 0 %" or a conservative default below 100 %, not a hard-coded physiological constant.
- Even a perfect choice of category leaves a large individual error: the Bajunaid prediction interval of about -25 % to +34 % for a typical adult (my arithmetic: predicted 10.5 MJ gives 6.3 to 16.8 MJ) is the honest uncertainty of any formula-based TDEE. This argues for showing the formula value as a starting estimate and replacing it by the own-data estimate as soon as that is available.
- For an "inactive office worker" check value, the Bajunaid equation (sea level, no activity input) can serve as a plausibility comparison of BMR x PAL, but not for athletes.

### Gaps

- No primary source and no validation study for the five multipliers 1.2/1.375/1.55/1.725/1.9 was found. They appear to be a convention of later practice attached to the Harris-Benedict equation; who set them and when remains unverified.
- No study was found that compares self-selected activity categories of consumer calculators with DLW-measured TEE in healthy adults; the cited evidence (Lichtman, Dhurandhar) concerns self-reported activity in general.
- NASEM 2023 equations, coefficients and standard errors were not read (only chapter excerpts).
- Net energy cost of walking per kg and km and the accuracy of step-to-kcal conversion specifically: no primary source verified. The figure of about 0.5 kcal/kg/km net is my estimate from general knowledge and must not be cited as a finding.
- Energy cost of resistance training (MET values of 3.5 to 6 in the Compendium, afterburn/EPOC) and its measurement error were not researched; the Compendium values were not read individually.
- The April 2025 correction to Bajunaid et al. was not checked; coefficients above are from the original full text.
- The exact wording of the 230 counts per minute break in Pontzer 2016 was read in the full text, but the corresponding kcal value of the plateau was not extracted.

## How large is the thermic effect of food per macronutrient, and does it matter for the calculation?

### Takeaway

Diet-induced thermogenesis is about 10 % of energy intake on a mixed diet, with a clear ranking protein (20 to 30 %) > carbohydrate (5 to 10 %) > fat (0 to 3 %). It is already contained in any PAL-based TDEE, so it needs no separate term; only a markedly higher protein share shifts expenditure by a few dozen kcal per day.

### Cited Findings

- Westerterp 2004 (Nutr Metab 1:5), review: reported DIT values are 0 to 3 % for fat, 5 to 10 % for carbohydrate, 20 to 30 % for protein and 10 to 30 % for alcohol; with a mixed diet in energy balance DIT is about 10 % of the energy ingested (5 to 15 % of daily expenditure across studies). Verified (full text). [consensus] [Westerterp 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15507147/)
- Bray et al. 2012 (JAMA 307:47-55), inpatient overfeeding by 954 kcal/d for 8 weeks, n = 25, diets with 5 %, 15 % or 25 % protein: weight gain 3.16 kg (low protein) vs 6.05 and 6.51 kg; body fat gain was similar in all groups; resting energy expenditure rose by 160 and 227 kcal/d with normal and high protein and not with low protein; lean mass changed by +2.87 and +3.18 kg with normal and high protein (loss with low protein). Verified (abstract). [Bray 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)
- PAL values from DLW are ratios of total expenditure (which includes DIT) to BMR, so the factorial approach TEE = PAL x BMR of FAO/WHO/UNU includes DIT. [FAO/WHO/UNU 2001](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e07.htm)

### Inferences

- No separate TEF term in the app: BMR x PAL already contains about 10 % DIT. Adding "TEF 10 %" on top would count it twice.
- Size of a protein effect, own arithmetic from Westerterp's ranges: replacing 100 g carbohydrate by 100 g protein (400 kcal each) raises DIT from about 20 to 40 kcal to about 80 to 120 kcal, a difference of roughly 60 to 80 kcal/d. Going from 15 % to 30 % protein at 2000 kcal is about +45 to +60 kcal/d. This is below the error of every other term and not worth modelling; it is a (small) argument for higher protein targets in a deficit.
- The 4/4/9 Atwater factors used for food labels are metabolizable energy and do not include DIT, so net available energy from protein is lower than labelled. An own-data TDEE estimate absorbs this automatically.

### Gaps

- No meta-analysis quantifying the between-person variability of DIT, or the effect of meal size, processing and fiber, was fetched.
- Whether DIT is reduced in obesity or after weight loss is debated; not researched here.

## How valid is the 7700 kcal/kg (3500 kcal/lb) rule, and what should replace it?

### Takeaway

7700 kcal/kg approximates the energy content of lost body tissue for people with substantial fat stores and moderate, fat-dominated loss, but as a static predictor ("500 kcal/d deficit gives 0.5 kg per week indefinitely") it clearly overestimates long-term weight change, because expenditure falls with body weight; for lean people and early (water-dominated) phases the true energy per kg is lower. The scientific consensus replaces the static rule with dynamic models (NIH Body Weight Planner, Pennington calculator).

### Cited Findings

- Wishnofsky 1958 (Am J Clin Nutr 6:542-546) derived "3500 kcal per pound": from the 59-day very low calorie diet studies of Strang et al. 1930 (-2100 kcal/d and -0.6 lb/d) and from adipose tissue composition (Bozenraad: 3700 kcal/lb). The rule assumes constant intake, no change in energy expenditure, and that weight loss consists of adipose tissue. Verified (via full text of Thomas 2014). [Wishnofsky 1958](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13594881/); [Thomas 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24699137/)
- Hall 2008 (Int J Obes 32:573-576): 3500 kcal/lb equals 32.2 MJ/kg. Using a modified Forbes equation, the cumulative deficit required per kg lost is larger with greater initial body fat; the rule approximately matches the predicted energy density of lost weight in obese people with initial body fat above 30 kg and overestimates the deficit needed per kg for people with less body fat; with ongoing loss the average energy per kg falls, because a greater share comes from lean mass with low energy density; this may explain why men lose more weight than women at the same deficit. Verified (abstract). [Hall 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- Energy densities used in Hall's models: body fat 39.5 MJ/kg (about 9440 kcal/kg), lean tissue change 7.6 MJ/kg (about 1820 kcal/kg); worked example of the 2008 paper: 15 kg loss starting from 20 kg body fat gives 24.7 MJ per kg (about 5900 kcal/kg). Snippet level (search excerpts of the full text and of the Lancet web appendix), not read in the primary text. [Hall, Lancet web appendix](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/BWP/Hall_Lancet_Web_Appendix.pdf)
- Hall et al. 2011 (Lancet 378:826-837): dynamic model validated against feeding studies. New rule of thumb for the average overweight adult: every permanent change of energy intake of 100 kJ/d leads to an eventual weight change of about 1 kg (about 10 kcal/d per pound, about 24 kcal/d per kg), with half of the change reached in about 1 year and 95 % in about 3 years. Example: a 100 kg man who cuts 2 MJ/d (480 kcal/d) plateaus near 75 kg rather than losing indefinitely; variability between individuals a few kg. The static 3500 kcal rule ignores the fall of resting and activity expenditure with weight. Verified (full text). [consensus] [Hall 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21872751/); tool: [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/bwp)
- Thomas et al. 2013 (Int J Obes 37:1611-1613): in 7 supervised weight loss experiments the 3500 kcal rule "grossly overestimates" actual weight loss; dynamic alternatives are available as web and spreadsheet tools from Pennington Biomedical. Verified (abstract). [Thomas 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23628852/); comment by [Hall and Chow 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23774459/)
- Thomas et al. 2014 (J Acad Nutr Diet 114:857-861): "Wishnofsky's Rule as applied is inaccurate"; it remains in textbooks and on major health websites. Verified (full text opening). [Thomas 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24699137/)
- Heymsfield et al. 2014 (Obes Rev 15:310-321): the "quarter FFM rule" (25 % of lost weight is fat-free mass) is an oversimplification; the FFM share depends on initial fatness, deficit size, protein, exercise, sex and time. Verified (abstract). [Heymsfield 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24447775/)
- Bhutani et al. 2017 (Physiol Rep 5:e13336): short-term free-living weight changes (2 weeks, 1 to 3 kg) consist of 84 % fat-free mass (mostly water); their energy density is only about 2380 kcal/kg. Verified (abstract). [single study] [Bhutani 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28676555/)
- Weight gain: Bray et al. 2012: the same 954 kcal/d surplus produced 3.16 to 6.51 kg in 8 weeks depending on protein, with similar fat gain, so kcal per kg gained varied about twofold. [Bray 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)
- Levine et al. 1999 (Science 283:212-214): 16 nonobese adults overfed by 1000 kcal/d for 8 weeks: fat gain varied tenfold between individuals. [Levine 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880251/)
- Slater et al. 2019 (Front Nutr 6:131), review: the energy surplus needed to maximize muscle hypertrophy is unknown and the commonly recommended surpluses have never been validated; the authors suggest conservative surpluses. Verified (abstract). [Slater 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31482093/)

### Inferences

- Two different uses must be kept apart. (a) Converting a weekly weight goal into a daily deficit for the coming weeks: 7700 kcal/kg is a reasonable upper-range constant for overweight users losing mainly fat; for lean users the real value is lower (Hall's example: about 5900 kcal/kg), so they lose somewhat faster than planned at first, with a larger lean share. (b) Projecting weight months ahead from a fixed intake: here the static rule fails; expenditure must be recomputed from the current weight.
- The app already softens (b) if it recalculates BMR (Mifflin: 10 kcal per kg) and the MET credits from the current weight: per kg lost, TDEE falls by about 10 x PAL = 12 to 19 kcal/d from the formula alone, which is in the same direction as, but smaller than, Hall's steady-state value of about 24 kcal/d per kg. A forecast that updates the target with each new weight therefore approximates a dynamic model; a forecast that extrapolates a constant deficit linearly does not.
- Simple alternative rule for long-range statements in the UI: "a permanent change of about 24 kcal/d (100 kJ) changes body weight by about 1 kg in the long run; half of it after about a year". Useful for maintenance planning: after losing 10 kg, expect a maintenance need about 240 kcal/d lower.
- Composition-aware constant, own arithmetic from Hall's densities: energy per kg = f x 9440 + (1 - f) x 1820 kcal, with f the fat share of the weight change. f = 0.75 gives about 7535 kcal/kg (close to 7700), f = 0.6 gives about 6390, f = 0.5 about 5630. For lean muscle gain (mostly lean tissue) the stored energy per kg is far below 7700, but the cost of building it is not known (Slater), so a symmetric 7700 kcal/kg for a gain goal has no empirical basis; it is merely conservative for fat gain.
- The own-data expenditure estimate (weight trend x 7700) inherits the density problem: in the first one to two weeks of a diet or after a change in carbohydrate or salt intake, weight change is mostly water (about 2400 kcal/kg, Bhutani), so trend x 7700 overstates the true energy imbalance up to threefold. This argues for the 21-day window (or longer) and for distrusting estimates right after a diet start.

### Gaps

- Hall 2008 full text (equations, the 39.5 and 7.6 MJ/kg values, the 24.7 MJ/kg example) could not be read directly (only abstract and search excerpts); these three numbers should be checked against the paper before being hard-coded.
- The Pennington model equations (Thomas et al. 2011) were not fetched; only the 2013 and 2014 papers.
- Stored water per gram of glycogen (commonly given as 3 to 4 g) has no verified source here.
- No verified numbers for the energy cost of lean mass accretion in resistance-trained adults; Slater 2019 states that it is unknown.
- How well the Body Weight Planner predicts individuals (not group means) in free-living users was not researched beyond the Lancet paper's statement on variability.

## How large is adaptive thermogenesis (and NEAT change), and is it relevant for a tracker?

### Takeaway

Adaptive thermogenesis exists but in well-controlled studies it is small in resting metabolism: about 50 to 100 kcal/d beyond what the loss of body mass explains, appearing within days of restriction and largely fading after weight stabilisation. Larger falls in total expenditure (several hundred kcal/d) are reported for total rather than resting expenditure and for extreme cases; they come mainly from the non-resting component (less and cheaper movement), and long-term persistence is contested.

### Cited Findings

- Leibel, Rosenbaum, Hirsch 1995 (NEJM 332:621-628), 18 obese and 23 never obese inpatients: maintaining a body weight 10 % below usual lowered total energy expenditure by 6 ± 3 (never obese) and 8 ± 5 (obese) kcal per kg FFM per day beyond the change expected from body composition; a 10 % weight gain raised it by 9 ± 7 and 8 ± 4 kcal/kg FFM/d. Verified (abstract). [Leibel 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7632212/)
- Rosenbaum et al. 2008 (Am J Clin Nutr 88:906-912): the reduction in expenditure persisted in people who had maintained a reduced weight for more than 1 year (small matched groups). Verified (abstract). [contested] [Rosenbaum 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18842775/); review: [Rosenbaum and Leibel 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20935667/)
- Müller et al. 2015 (Am J Clin Nutr 102:807-819), 32 nonobese men, 3 weeks at 50 % of energy need: -6.0 kg; REE fell by 266 kcal/d, of which 108 kcal/d (about 48 % of the fall in adjusted terms) was adaptive thermogenesis, 72 kcal/d after accounting for organ composition; the adaptation was significant within 3 days. Verified (abstract). [Müller 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26399868/); review: [Müller, Enderle, Bosy-Westphal 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27739007/)
- Martins et al. 2020 (Am J Clin Nutr 112:558-565), 171 overweight women, about 12 kg loss: adaptive thermogenesis in RMR -54 ± 105 kcal/d right after weight loss (after stabilisation), no longer present at 1 and 2 years, and no predictor of weight regain. Verified (abstract). [Martins 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32386226/)
- Martins et al. 2022 (Obesity 30:400-406): adaptive thermogenesis of -46 ± 113 kcal/d during the diet was associated with less weight loss and a longer time to reach the goal. Verified (abstract). [Martins 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35088553/)
- Nunes et al. 2022 (Br J Nutr 127:451-469), systematic review, 33 studies, 2528 participants: adaptive thermogenesis was reported in 27 studies, but values were small or non-significant in the better designed studies and were attenuated or absent after a period of weight stabilisation. Verified (abstract). [consensus on small size, contested on persistence] [Nunes 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33762040/)
- Fothergill et al. 2016 (Obesity 24:1612-1619), 14 "Biggest Loser" participants: mean loss 58.3 kg; RMR fell by 610 kcal/d; six years later, despite substantial regain, metabolic adaptation was -499 ± 207 kcal/d. Verified (abstract). [single extreme cohort] [Fothergill 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136388/)
- Hall 2022 (Obesity 30:11-13) reinterprets this persistent adaptation as a possible compensation for the large sustained increase in physical activity (constrained energy model), not as a consequence of weight loss alone. Verified (abstract). [Hall 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34816627/)
- Redman et al. 2009, CALERIE phase 1 (PLoS One 4:e4377), n = 48 overweight adults, 25 % restriction: total daily expenditure adjusted for body composition fell by 431 ± 51 kcal/d at 3 months and 240 ± 83 kcal/d at 6 months, together with reduced physical activity. Verified (abstract). [Redman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19198647/)
- Byrne et al. 2018, MATADOR (Int J Obes 42:129-138), obese men: intermittent restriction (2 weeks diet, 2 weeks energy balance) led to a smaller fall in adjusted REE than continuous restriction (-360 vs -749 kJ/d, about -86 vs -179 kcal/d) and more fat loss. Verified (abstract). [single trial] [Byrne 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/)
- Trexler, Smith-Ryan, Norton 2014 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:7), narrative review for athletes: lists reduced RMR, NEAT, TEF and exercise cost as components of adaptation during contest dieting and recommends moderate deficits; no pooled effect sizes. [Trexler 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24571926/)
- NEAT: Levine et al. 1999 (Science 283:212-214), 16 nonobese adults, +1000 kcal/d for 8 weeks: two thirds of the increase in total expenditure came from non-exercise activity thermogenesis; the change in NEAT predicted resistance to fat gain (r = 0.77); fat gain differed tenfold. Verified (abstract). [Levine 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880251/)

### Inferences

- For the formula path, a hard-coded "metabolic adaptation" discount on RMR is not justified: the typical size (about 50 to 100 kcal/d, SD about 100) is smaller than the equation's own error and disappears with weight stabilisation.
- The practically relevant fall is in total expenditure: lower body mass (already covered when BMR and MET credits use current weight), lower DIT from less food (about 10 % of the intake reduction, so about 50 kcal/d for a 500 kcal deficit), and less spontaneous movement. In CALERIE this added up to 240 to 430 kcal/d below prediction. A user in a prolonged deficit can therefore see real loss rates noticeably below the planned ones without any logging error.
- This is the strongest argument for the own-data expenditure estimate: it measures the adapted TDEE instead of assuming it. A sensible presentation: when the own-data value lies clearly below the formula value during a diet, that is expected physiology (and/or under-reporting), not a defect.
- For lean, strength-training users in long deficits (the Trexler population) the adaptations are relatively larger, but the evidence is narrative; no number should be built in.
- A daily step count is the only cheap proxy for NEAT decline that the app has; a falling step trend during a diet is a meaningful signal.

### Gaps

- Minnesota Starvation Experiment figures (Keys 1950; later reanalyses by Dulloo) were not fetched; no number is cited.
- No verified pooled estimate of the fall in NEAT or step counts during dieting.
- Effect of diet breaks and refeeds beyond MATADOR (for example the ICECAP trial in resistance-trained people) was not researched.
- Persistence beyond 1 to 2 years is contested (Rosenbaum 2008 vs Martins 2020 and Nunes 2022); the positive long-term studies are small.

## How well can TDEE be estimated from logged intake and the body weight trend (adaptive TDEE)?

### Takeaway

The method is physically sound (energy balance) and validated in the inverse direction: from frequent weighings, mathematical models recover intake with a mean error of about 40 kcal/d and an individual error of about 215 kcal/d. Its limits are the length of the window (roughly four weeks of frequent weights for a 95 % interval under 300 kcal/d), water fluctuations in short windows, and above all intake under-reporting, which shifts the estimated TDEE downward by the same amount (typically 10 to 20 % in studies, about 200 kcal/d for app-based records).

### Cited Findings

- Hall and Chow 2011 (Am J Clin Nutr 94:66-74): estimating changes in free-living energy intake from body weight time courses requires repeated weight measurements over more than 28 days to obtain a 95 % confidence interval below 300 kcal/d; daily weight noise dominates shorter windows. Verified (abstract). [Hall and Chow 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21562087/)
- Sanghvi et al. 2015 (Am J Clin Nutr 102:353-358), n = 140 CALERIE participants over 2 years: intake calculated by a mathematical model from repeated body weights agreed with the DLW/DXA intake-balance method within 40 kcal/d on average; individual root mean square deviation 215 kcal/d. Verified (abstract). [Sanghvi 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26040640/)
- Turicchi et al. 2020 (PLoS One 15:e0232152), smart scale data of 1421 adults: body weight fluctuates within the week by about 0.35 % (higher after the weekend, lowest on Friday) and rises by about 1.35 % over Christmas. Verified (abstract). [Turicchi 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32353079/)
- Bhutani et al. 2017: short-term weight changes of 1 to 3 kg are 84 % fat-free mass (water) with an energy density of about 2380 kcal/kg, far below 7700. Verified (abstract). [Bhutani 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28676555/)
- Under-reporting, classic methods: OPEN study, Subar et al. 2003 (Am J Epidemiol 158:1-13), n = 484 with DLW: energy under-reporting on 24 h recalls 12 to 14 % in men and 16 to 20 % in women; on food frequency questionnaires 31 to 36 % and 34 to 38 %. Verified (abstract). [Subar 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12835280/)
- Burrows et al. 2019 (Front Endocrinol 10:850), systematic review, 59 studies, 6298 adults: the majority of studies found significant under-reporting of energy intake against DLW, more with higher BMI. Verified (abstract). [Burrows 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31920966/)
- Bajunaid et al. 2025: in national surveys only 43.7 % of adult NHANES reports and 36.6 % (men) and 38.1 % (women) of NDNS reports lie within the 95 % prediction interval of TEE; below the lower limit are 50.4 % (men) and 53.6 % (women) in NHANES and 62.8 % and 61.5 % in NDNS; the mean discrepancy is 3.5 MJ/d in NDNS and 1.8 MJ/d (men) and 2.8 MJ/d (women) in NHANES; no discrepancy at BMI 15 to 20, growing with BMI; under-reporters report a higher protein share and a lower fat share. Verified (full text). [Bajunaid 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39806218/)
- App-based records: Zhang et al. 2021 (Adv Nutr 12:2321-2332), meta-analysis: dietary record apps underestimate energy intake by 202 kcal/d (95 % CI -319 to -85) against traditional methods; only 57 kcal/d when app and reference use the same food composition table. Verified (abstract). [Zhang 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34019624/)
- Lozano et al. 2024 (Am J Clin Nutr 120:419-430): in a laboratory meal test MyFitnessPal overestimated energy by 7.0 % on average. Diktas et al. 2025 (J Nutr 155:4296-4306): in free living with provided foods the mean percent error of MyFitnessPal was 3.7 %. Verified (abstracts). [Lozano 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38825184/); [Diktas 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41022156/)
- Urban et al. 2011 (JAMA 306:287-293), 269 restaurant items: stated energy was accurate on average (+10 kcal per portion), but 19 % of items contained at least 100 kcal more than stated. Verified (abstract). [Urban 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21771989/)
- Lichtman et al. 1992: extreme case, intake under-reported by 47 ± 16 %. [Lichtman 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/)

### Inferences

- The app's design (21 days, at least 14 complete days, at least 4 weigh-ins spanning 10 days, linear trend x 7700) is conceptually in line with the intake-balance literature, but on the lean side of what Hall and Chow require: 21 days with as few as 4 weigh-ins gives a wide confidence interval. Error propagation, own arithmetic: an error of 0.35 % of body weight (about 0.25 kg at 75 kg) in the trend difference over 21 days equals 0.25 x 7700 / 21 = about 90 kcal/d; with only 4 weigh-ins and real water swings of 0.5 to 1 kg the error is 180 to 370 kcal/d. Recommendations that follow: require more weigh-ins for a "confident" value (for example 8 to 10 in the window), show a range instead of a single number, extend the window to 28 days when data allow, and smooth the result over time (running average of successive estimates).
- The estimate is "expenditure as seen through the user's own logging": systematic under-reporting of x kcal/d lowers it by x. For setting a target this is a feature, not a bug, as long as the same logging habits continue (the bias cancels). It must not be presented as true physiological TDEE, and it breaks when logging completeness changes (hence the app's "complete days" rule is important).
- Typical size to expect: the own-data value will often be 100 to 300 kcal/d below the formula value even without adaptation (app records about -200 kcal/d; classic records 10 to 20 %), more with higher BMI.
- Using a fixed 7700 kcal/kg for the trend term is acceptable over 3 to 4 weeks of steady dieting but overstates the imbalance when the trend is water-driven (diet start, carbohydrate or sodium change, menstrual cycle, creatine). Plausibility guards (as the app has with "implausible") are warranted; a suppression of the estimate in the first 1 to 2 weeks after a large intake change would be consistent with Bhutani's finding.
- For lean users gaining weight with resistance training, the trend term is the least certain (energy density of gained tissue between about 1800 and 9400 kcal/kg depending on composition), so the estimate is less reliable in a surplus than in a deficit.
- Menstrual cycle related water retention makes a 28-day window (one full cycle) preferable to 21 days for many women (see Gaps for the missing source).

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed validation of consumer "adaptive TDEE" algorithms (MacroFactor, Carbon and similar) against DLW was found; the cited validations concern the research models of Hall and Thomas.
- Size of weight fluctuation across the menstrual cycle: no source verified here.
- Minimum number of weigh-ins per week for a given precision: Hall and Chow's abstract gives only the 28-day statement; the detailed dependence on weighing frequency was not extracted.
- Whether trend smoothing methods (exponential moving average vs linear regression) differ in accuracy has, as far as found, not been studied in the peer-reviewed literature.
- Under-reporting specifically in motivated long-term app users (as opposed to survey respondents) is poorly quantified; the two MyFitnessPal studies are short and partly use provided foods.

## What do DGE/D-A-CH (2015) and EFSA (2013) give as energy reference values?

### Takeaway

Both bodies use the same structure as the app (resting expenditure x PAL) but with PAL steps 1.4/1.6/1.8 (EFSA also 2.0) that describe total activity, and both stress that the values are group guidance and that the individual control variable is body weight. For adults 25 to 50 years at reference weight (BMI 22) DGE gives 2300/2700/3000 kcal (men) and 1800/2100/2400 kcal (women).

### Cited Findings

- DGE/D-A-CH guideline values for energy intake (derivation 2015; publication Ann Nutr Metab 2015;66:219-223), kcal/d at PAL 1.4 / 1.6 / 1.8. Men: 19 to <25 y 2400/2800/3100; 25 to <51 y 2300/2700/3000; 51 to <65 y 2200/2500/2800; ≥65 y 2100/2500/2800. Women: 19 to <25 y 1900/2200/2500; 25 to <51 y 1800/2100/2400; 51 to <65 y 1700/2000/2200; ≥65 y 1700/1900/2100. Verified (fetched page). [DGE Referenzwerte Energie](https://www.dge.de/wissenschaft/referenzwerte/energie/)
- Reference persons of these values: adult heights are medians from DEGS1, weights calculated for BMI 22 kg/m²: men 25 to <51 y 179.2 cm and 70.7 kg, women 165.1 cm and 60.0 kg; men 19 to <25 y 179.4 cm and 70.8 kg, women 165.8 cm and 60.5 kg; ≥65 y 174.2 cm and 66.8 kg (men), 161.1 cm and 57.1 kg (women). The values need individual adjustment for people outside the normal weight range or with low activity; the actual body weight is the decisive control parameter. Verified (fetched page). [DGE Referenzwerte Energie](https://www.dge.de/wissenschaft/referenzwerte/energie/)
- DGE additions: pregnancy +250 kcal/d in the 2nd and +500 kcal/d in the 3rd trimester (normal weight before pregnancy, unchanged activity); exclusive breastfeeding +500 kcal/d in the first 4 to 6 months. Energy factors per the EU labelling rules: protein 4 kcal/g, fat 9, carbohydrate 4, fiber 2, alcohol 7. Verified (fetched page). [DGE Referenzwerte Energie](https://www.dge.de/wissenschaft/referenzwerte/energie/)
- EFSA 2013: average requirements for energy = REE predicted with the Henry 2005 equations x PAL of 1.4, 1.6, 1.8 and 2.0, corresponding to low active (sedentary), moderately active, active and very active lifestyles; reference body weights for BMI 22; the values are for groups, not individuals. Verified (EFSA page). [EFSA 2013](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/3005)
- FAO/WHO/UNU 2001: lifestyle PAL ranges 1.40 to 1.69, 1.70 to 1.99, 2.00 to 2.40 with Schofield BMR. Verified. [FAO/WHO/UNU 2001](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e07.htm)
- Henry 2005: the Oxford equations give lower BMR than Schofield for adult men up to 60 y and all adult women, which is why EFSA chose them. [Henry 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16277825/)

### Inferences

- Cross-check of the app's method against DGE, own arithmetic: man 30 y, 179.2 cm, 70.7 kg: Mifflin = 707 + 1120 - 150 + 5 = 1682 kcal; x 1.4 = 2355, x 1.6 = 2691, x 1.8 = 3028, which matches the DGE values 2300/2700/3000 within rounding. Woman 30 y, 165.1 cm, 60.0 kg: Mifflin = 600 + 1032 - 150 - 161 = 1321 kcal; x 1.4 = 1849, x 1.6 = 2114, x 1.8 = 2378 vs DGE 1800/2100/2400. So Mifflin x DGE-PAL reproduces the German reference values; the app's formula base is consistent with them.
- The same check shows what the app's lowest factor does: 1.2 x 1682 = 2018 kcal for the reference man, about 300 kcal below the lowest DGE and EFSA value (PAL 1.4). If the user then logs no trainings, the app's budget for a sedentary person is likely about 15 % too low compared with the reference bodies. This is conservative for weight loss but wrong as a maintenance statement.
- The mapping problem: DGE/EFSA PAL values include sport; the app's factors are meant without sport. A consistent mapping would be non-exercise PAL about 1.4 (sitting), 1.5 to 1.6 (mixed), 1.7 to 1.8 (standing or walking work), about 2.0 (heavy work), plus net training calories. FAO's example gives the scale for sport: one hour of moderate to vigorous exercise per day raises PAL by about 0.2 (1.55 to 1.75).
- The DGE table is useful in the UI as a plausibility anchor ("Richtwert DGE für Ihre Altersgruppe") but not as a personal target, because it is tied to BMI 22 reference weights.

### Gaps

- The DGE page does not state which resting expenditure equation underlies the table, nor the PAL examples per occupation (PAL 1.2 for bed-bound, 1.4 to 1.5 office work and so on) or the supplement of 0.3 PAL units for regular sport that the printed D-A-CH reference values contain. These details are known from memory only and were not verified.
- The secondary claim "Stand 2024" for the DGE energy table could not be confirmed; the DGE page itself says derivation 2015.
- EFSA's tabulated kcal values per age group were not extracted (only the method).
- NASEM 2023 values were not compared numerically.
