## 1. Minimum and recommended fat intake (essential fatty acids, reference values, sports nutrition floors, sex hormones)

### Takeaway

All reference bodies converge on a fat range of roughly 20 to 35 % of energy (DGE guideline value 30 %, 35 % for active people; WHO upper bound 30 %), and sports nutrition bodies (ACSM/AND/DC 2016, DGE working group sports nutrition 2020) name 20 % of energy as the floor; the strict physiological minimum from essential fatty acids is far lower (about 3 to 4.5 % of energy, roughly 7 to 20 g/day). The hormone argument for a fat floor rests on a small, old evidence base (6 crossover studies, 206 men, SMD about -0.4), so a floor is prudent but its exact height (0.5 vs 1.0 g/kg) is convention, and in an energy deficit a g/kg floor (about 0.5 to 0.6 g/kg, absolute minimum near 0.5) is more robust than a percentage.

### Cited Findings

Reference values (general population)

- EFSA NDA Panel 2010 (EFSA Journal 8(3):1461, doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1461): reference intake range for total fat 20 to 35 E% for adults; Adequate Intake linoleic acid (LA) 4 E%, alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) 0.5 E%, EPA+DHA 250 mg/day for adults (cardiovascular basis); no average requirement could be set for ALA. - IOM/Health Canada DRI 2005, adults 19 to 50 (verified from the Health Canada table): AMDR total fat 20 to 35 % of energy, carbohydrate 45 to 65 %, protein 10 to 35 %; n-6 (LA) 5 to 10 %, n-3 (ALA) 0.6 to 1.2 %; AI linoleic acid 17 g/day men, 12 g/day women; AI alpha-linolenic acid 1.6 g/day men, 1.1 g/day women; saturated and trans fat "as low as possible while consuming a nutritionally adequate diet". ### Inferences

- Essential fatty acid floor in grams: DGE 2.5 + 0.5 = 3 En% is about 6.7 g at 2 000 kcal; EFSA 4 + 0.5 = 4.5 E% is 10 g at 2 000 kcal; IOM AIs are 13 to 19 g (LA + ALA). Real foods deliver these acids mixed with other fat, so the EFA requirement is met in practice only at a clearly higher total fat. A total fat floor of 20 En% (44 g at 2 000 kcal, 33 g at 1 500 kcal) covers it with a wide margin; this is why 20 % is the consensus floor (EFSA, IOM, ACSM, DGE sports group).
- Percent vs g/kg: at maintenance, 20 to 35 % of energy maps to about 0.5 to 1.5 g/kg (Iraki). In a deficit the percentage shrinks in absolute terms: 25 % of 1 500 kcal is 42 g, which is 0.46 g/kg for a 90 kg person and 0.69 g/kg for 60 kg. A percentage rule therefore undershoots exactly for heavy people on low calories. No authoritative body publishes a g/kg minimum (the DGE sports group says explicitly that none exists), so any g/kg floor is a pragmatic convention from the bodybuilding literature. Suggested calculation rule: fat = max(preset share of energy, floor g/kg × body weight), with floor 0.5 g/kg (hard minimum, about the lower end of Iraki) and a soft warning below about 0.6 to 0.7 g/kg or below 20 % of energy when held for long. For people with obesity a floor per kg of total body weight overshoots; using a reference weight (for example weight at BMI 25) or capping the floor is sensible, but this is my inference, not something a source states.
- The current defaults (30 % general, 25 % diet) are inside every reference range. 30 % equals the DGE guideline value and the WHO upper bound. Helms 2014 supports going to 15 to 20 % in an aggressive, high protein deficit when carbohydrates are otherwise too low for training, as a temporary state.
- Evidence strength: 20 to 35 % as an acceptable range is consensus. "Fat below about 20 % lowers testosterone in men" is weak to moderate (small crossover trials, effect about 10 to 15 %, mostly within the normal range, clinical relevance for muscle gain unproven). For women there is no intervention evidence of comparable quality that low fat at adequate energy disturbs the cycle; the robust finding concerns low energy availability. An app should therefore guard against too low calories at least as much as against too low fat.
- The "low carb" preset (carbohydrates fixed at 20 % of energy, fat fills) can push fat above 50 % of energy; that leaves the DGE/WHO/EFSA ranges and typically also the saturated fat target. This is acceptable as a personal choice (see section 2) but the saturated fat and fiber targets become the relevant guard rails.

### Gaps

- EFSA 2010 fat opinion and the ACSM 2016 position were not read in the original this session (numbers confirmed through secondary summaries only).
- I could not verify a quantitative minimum of dietary fat needed for absorption of fat soluble vitamins (figures like "a few grams per meal" circulate, but I found no primary source in this session). The DGE sports paper and ACSM name vitamin absorption as a reason for the 20 % floor without quantifying it.
- Hämäläinen 1984 and Dorgan 1996 were not read individually; they are covered only as included studies of the Whittaker meta-analysis. Their individual designs, sample sizes and effect sizes are therefore not reported here.
- ISSN (Kerksick et al. 2018, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 15:38, doi:10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y, [PMC6090881](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6090881/)): the paper exists, but I could not verify its fat numbers (often quoted as about 30 % of energy, 0.5 to 1 g/kg as a lower bound during fat loss). Treat those figures as unverified.
- Content of the 2026 corrigendum to Whittaker & Wu is unknown.
- The upper bound of 40 En% fat in the DGE/ÖGE planning table was seen only in a search summary.

## 2. Low carb vs low fat at equal calories and protein

### Takeaway

With calories and protein matched, the fat to carbohydrate ratio has no practically relevant effect on fat loss or energy expenditure (controlled feeding: 26 kcal/day and 16 g fat/day in favour of lower fat; free living 12 months: 5.3 vs 6.0 kg, not significant), so the ratio is largely personal preference and adherence; the one exception is ketogenic levels of carbohydrate, which tend to cost lean mass in people who train with weights.

### Cited Findings

- Hall KD, Guo J. "Obesity energetics: body weight regulation and the effects of diet composition." Gastroenterology 2017;152(7):1718-1727 (verified from PMC): meta-analysis of 32 controlled feeding studies, 563 participants, isocaloric substitution of carbohydrate for fat with equal protein, all food provided; carbohydrate ranged 1 to 83 % and fat 4 to 84 % of energy. Pooled: energy expenditure 26 kcal/day higher (P < .0001) and body fat loss 16 g/day greater (P < .0001) with the lower fat diets. Direction is opposite to the carbohydrate-insulin model; the authors judge the effects too small to matter ("a calorie is a calorie" for practical purposes). No pooled confidence intervals are given in the text. [PMC5568065](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568065); [PubMed 28193517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28193517/)
- Hall KD et al. "Calorie for calorie, dietary fat restriction results in more body fat loss than carbohydrate restriction in people with obesity." Cell Metab 2015: metabolic ward crossover, 19 adults with obesity, 5 day baseline then 6 days of selective restriction; carbohydrate restriction 53 ± 6 g/day body fat loss, fat restriction 89 ± 6 g/day (by fat balance), despite lower insulin on the reduced carbohydrate diet. [NIH news release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-study-finds-cutting-dietary-fat-reduces-body-fat-more-cutting-carbs); [paper record](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/3344851/calorie-for-calorie-dietary-fat-restriction-results-in-more-body-fat-loss-than-carbohydrate-restriction-in-people-with-obesity)
- Hall KD et al. Am J Clin Nutr 2016 (isocaloric ketogenic diet, metabolic ward, men with overweight or obesity): energy expenditure in the chamber rose by 57 ± 13 kcal/day (P = 0.0004), sleeping EE by 89 ± 14 kcal/day, effect waning over time; doubly labelled water showed 151 ± 63 kcal/day (P = 0.03); body fat loss slowed during the ketogenic phase. [PMC4962163](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4962163)
- Gardner CD et al. DIETFITS. JAMA 2018;319(7):667-679, doi:10.1001/jama.2018.0245: RCT, 609 adults aged 18 to 50, BMI 28 to 40, no diabetes; healthy low fat vs healthy low carbohydrate for 12 months; weight change -5.3 kg vs -6.0 kg, difference 0.7 kg (95 % CI -0.2 to 1.6), not significant; no diet × genotype interaction (P = .20) and no diet × insulin secretion (INS-30) interaction (P = .47); 481 completed; individual changes ranged from about -30 to +10 kg in both arms. [JAMA record](https://jamanetwork.altmetric.com/details/33436659?cmp=1); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180220123124.htm)
- Hall KD et al. "Effect of a plant-based, low-fat diet versus an animal-based, ketogenic diet on ad libitum energy intake." Nat Med 2021, doi:10.1038/s41591-020-01209-1: inpatient crossover, 20 adults, 2 weeks per diet, ad libitum; low fat (10.3 % fat, 75.2 % carbohydrate) led to 689 ± 73 kcal/day lower intake than the ketogenic diet (75.8 % fat, 10.0 % carbohydrate) over 2 weeks (544 ± 68 kcal/day in the last week), with no difference in reported hunger, fullness or enjoyment; the ketogenic diet had about twice the energy density. [Paper record](https://oar.a-star.edu.sg/communities-collections/articles/16959); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20210309/lower-energy-intake-observed-in-lowfat-vegan-vs-lowcarb-ketogenic-diet)
- The debate: Ludwig DS et al. "The carbohydrate-insulin model: a physiological perspective on the obesity pandemic." Am J Clin Nutr 2021;114(6):1873-1885, doi:10.1093/ajcn/nqab270 argues that high glycaemic load drives fat storage and thereby hunger; Hall and colleagues answered in 2022 with the energy balance model; Ludwig and Ebbeling argue the feeding studies are too short and misinterpreted. A free living feeding trial by Ebbeling et al. 2018 (BMJ) reported about 250 kcal/day higher total energy expenditure (doubly labelled water) on low vs high carbohydrate during weight loss maintenance; the method and analysis are disputed (Hall, Guo, Speakman 2019). Status: contested; the tightly controlled evidence does not support a metabolic advantage of low carbohydrate. [Ludwig 2021 record](https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid34515299); [Ludwig & Ebbeling, PMC6082688](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6082688/); [Hall, Guo, Speakman 2019 record](https://aura-test.abdn.ac.uk/handle/2164/13120); [MDedge on the debate](https://mdedge.com/content/do-carbs-drive-obesity-evidence-inconclusive-debate-continues)
- Ketogenic diets and lean mass with resistance training: Ashtary-Larky D et al. Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr 2022 (Epub 2021), doi:10.1080/10408398.2021.1890689: meta-analysis of 13 RCTs, 244 participants; ketogenic vs non ketogenic diets with resistance training reduced fat free mass (WMD -1.26 kg, 95 % CI -1.82 to -0.70) along with body mass and fat mass; conclusion: possible negative effect on FFM that resistance training does not offset. [PubMed 33624538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33624538/)
- Contrasting: Vargas-Molina S et al. Int J Environ Res Public Health 2022 (systematic review and meta-analysis, 111 resistance trained participants, ketogenic diet without programmed energy restriction): no significant difference in fat free mass between groups. [PMC9564904](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9564904)
- Vargas S et al. J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2018, doi:10.1186/s12970-018-0236-9: RCT, 24 trained men, 8 weeks of resistance training in an intended surplus; the ketogenic group lost fat mass and visceral fat without a significant change in lean mass, the non ketogenic group gained lean mass; authors: a ketogenic diet "might not be useful to increase muscle mass". [JISSN 2018](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-018-0236-9)
- Kephart WC et al. Sports (Basel) 2018: 12 week pilot in CrossFit trainees, ketogenic n = 7 vs control n = 5; DXA fat mass -12.4 % in the ketogenic group (P = 0.053), total lean mass not different, leg lean mass and vastus lateralis thickness trended down (fluid shifts possible), performance not impaired, LDL cholesterol rose. Very small, not randomised in a strict sense. [Sports 2018 record](https://doaj.org/article/f4a6aa4427a04d22955cf643693fe6cf)
- Iraki 2019: longitudinal studies in resistance trained people on ketogenic diets found less muscle gain than moderate or lower fat diets with ample carbohydrate. [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710)
- DGE 2011: evidence-based guidelines give no basis for an optimal fat to carbohydrate ratio for disease prevention; a carbohydrate share below 50 EN% is acceptable when essential nutrients are covered, cereal fiber (whole grain) remains a major part of fiber, saturated and trans fat do not rise and added protein comes from plants rather than red meat. [DGE-Position 2011 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/positionen/DGE-Positionspapier-Richtwerte-Energiezufuhr-KH-und-Fett.pdf)

### Inferences

- For an app: once calories and protein are fixed, the split between fat and carbohydrate can be a user preference within guard rails. Guard rails that the evidence supports: fat not below the floor of section 1; for people who train hard, carbohydrates not at ketogenic levels unless chosen deliberately (lean mass and performance), and a soft carbohydrate check against training volume (section 3).
- The measured metabolic differences (26 kcal/day) are an order of magnitude smaller than tracking error, so no preset should be advertised as burning more fat. Adherence decides: DIETFITS showed identical average results and a spread of 40 kg within each arm, with no usable predictor (genotype, insulin).
- Lean mass findings on ketogenic diets are partly confounded: ketogenic groups often ate fewer calories spontaneously (protein and fat satiety, restricted food choice) and glycogen plus bound water counts as fat free mass in DXA/BIA. The -1.26 kg FFM is therefore an upper estimate of real muscle loss. Fair wording: "probably somewhat worse for muscle gain, not proven harmful for muscle retention".
- A "low carb" preset at 20 % of energy carbohydrate (100 g at 2 000 kcal) is low carbohydrate but usually not ketogenic (< 50 g/day), so the ketogenic lean mass findings apply only partly.

### Gaps

- The Hall 2022 energy balance model paper and Ebbeling 2018 BMJ were confirmed only through secondary descriptions, not opened; journal and exact numbers of the 2022 paper are unverified.
- No pooled confidence intervals for Hall & Guo 2017 were found in the text.
- Long term (> 12 months) RCT comparisons with matched protein in lean, resistance trained people do not exist to my knowledge; evidence in this population is short (8 to 12 weeks) and small.
- Adherence as "the deciding factor" is the standard reading of DIETFITS and similar trials; I did not retrieve a dedicated meta-analysis on adherence (for example Dansinger 2005 or Johnston 2014), so no effect sizes are given.

## 3. Carbohydrate needs (minimum, by training load, resistance training)

### Takeaway

There is no essential dietary carbohydrate; 130 g/day (IOM RDA, brain glucose use) is a reference value, not a hard floor. Needs scale with training volume in g/kg (3-5, 5-7, 6-10, 8-12 g/kg for light to extreme endurance loads; same table in Burke 2011, ACSM 2016 and the DGE sports position 2019), while for typical resistance training (up to about 10 sets per muscle group, fed state) carbohydrate intake has little measurable effect on performance, so about 3 g/kg is a reasonable soft target rather than a requirement.

### Cited Findings

- IOM/Health Canada DRI 2005 (verified): carbohydrate EAR 100 g/day, RDA 130 g/day for adults; AMDR 45 to 65 % of energy; added sugars at most 25 % of energy. [Health Canada DRI tables](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/dietary-reference-intakes/tables/reference-values-macronutrients-dietary-reference-intakes-tables-2005.html)
- EFSA NDA Panel 2010 (EFSA Journal 8(3):1462, doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1462): reference intake range for carbohydrates 45 to 60 E% (adults and children over 1 year); only a range, no upper level; insufficient data to set an upper limit for (added) sugars; evidence on glycaemic index/load inconclusive. [EFSA opinion record](https://zendy.io/title/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1462)
- DGE: guideline value > 50 EN%, derived as remainder (100 minus 30 fat minus protein), not from a requirement; going below is acceptable under the conditions listed in section 2. [DGE-Position 2011 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/positionen/DGE-Positionspapier-Richtwerte-Energiezufuhr-KH-und-Fett.pdf)
- WHO 2023 carbohydrate guideline: no percentage target; carbohydrates should come mainly from whole grains, vegetables, fruits and pulses; adults at least 400 g vegetables and fruit and at least 25 g naturally occurring fiber per day. [WHO news release 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-07-2023-who-updates-guidelines-on-fats-and-carbohydrates)
- DGE working group sports nutrition: König D, Braun H, Carlsohn A, et al. "Kohlenhydrate in der Sporternährung", Ernährungs Umschau 2019;66(11):228-235, doi:10.4455/eu.2019.044 (table verified from the PDF, source of the table is Burke/ACSM): daily carbohydrate by load: low intensity 3-5 g/kg/day; moderate load (about 1 h/day) 5-7 g/kg/day; high, competitive endurance training (1 to 3 h/day) 6-10 g/kg/day; very high, extreme loads (more than 4 to 5 h/day) 8-12 g/kg/day. Further: glycogen refilling 7-12 g/kg over 24 h, loading 10-12 g/kg for 36 to 48 h, pre-exercise meal 1-4 g/kg, rapid refilling 1-1.2 g/kg per hour. [DGE Position Kohlenhydrate (PDF, KIT mirror)](https://www.ifss.kit.edu/rd_download/2.%20Forschung/Leistungsphysiologie%20und%20Ern%C3%A4hrung/Positionspapiere/Kohlenhydrate%20in%20der%20Sportern%C3%A4hrung.pdf)
- Burke LM, Hawley JA, Wong SHS, Jeukendrup AE. "Carbohydrates for training and competition." J Sports Sci 2011;29 Suppl 1:S17-S27, doi:10.1080/02640414.2011.585473: frames intake as "carbohydrate availability" relative to the fuel cost of the training rather than as percent of energy; during exercise 30-60 g/h, up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h. The g/kg table itself was verified only through the DGE paper and textbook reproductions that cite it. [PubMed 21660838](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016 uses the same tiers (3-5 g/kg light or skill based; 8-12 g/kg for 4 to 5 h/day moderate to high intensity confirmed in a reproduction; middle tiers confirmed via the DGE table). [Textbook reproduction of the table](https://med.libretexts.org/Courses/Chabot_College/Nutrition_1/12%3A_Performance_Nutrition/12.05%3A_Sports_Nutrition)
- Henselmans M, Bjørnsen T, Hedderman R, Vårvik FT. "The effect of carbohydrate intake on strength and resistance training performance: a systematic review." Nutrients 2022;14(4):856, doi:10.3390/nu14040856: 49 studies (19 acute, 6 glycogen depletion, 7 short term, 17 long term). Acute: no benefit of higher carbohydrate in 13, benefit in 6 (mostly fasted controls or sessions above 10 sets per muscle group). Long term: 15 of 17 no effect, 1 favoured higher, 1 lower carbohydrate. Conclusion: in the fed state and up to 10 sets per muscle group, carbohydrate intake per se is unlikely to affect strength training performance. [PubMed 35215506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35215506/); [MDPI full text](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/4/856)
- More open reading: King A et al. 2022 (Sports Med, systematic review and meta-analysis of acute carbohydrate feeding around resistance exercise) calls the evidence equivocal, with benefit more likely for long, high volume sessions and after fasting (secondary description). [Sports Med 2022 (PDF)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-022-01716-w.pdf)
- Escobar KA, VanDusseldorp TA, Kerksick CM. "Carbohydrate intake and resistance-based exercise: are current recommendations reflective of actual need?" Br J Nutr 2016;116:2053-2065: typical recommendations of 3-7 g/kg/day for resistance trainees stem largely from endurance research and anecdote; true glycogen depletion from resistance exercise is unlikely, so such intakes may not be necessary. [Paper record](https://digitalcommons.lindenwood.edu/faculty-research-papers/158)
- Slater G, Phillips SM. "Nutrition guidelines for strength sports: sprinting, weightlifting, throwing events, and bodybuilding." J Sports Sci 2011;29 Suppl 1:S67-S77, doi:10.1080/02640414.2011.574722: cited by Helms 2014 and Iraki 2019 for 4-7 g/kg/day in strength sports (figure verified through those citing papers, not in the original). [PMC4033492 (Helms citing it)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492); [paper record](https://www.peeref.com/works/12592225)
- Glycogen use in resistance sessions (as summarised by Iraki 2019): a single session lowers muscle glycogen by 24 to 40 %; glycolysis supplies about 80 % of ATP in a set of elbow flexion to failure; low glycogen reduced squat repetitions over three sets at 80 % 1RM; but loading with 7.7 g/kg/day for 48 h did not beat 0.37 g/kg/day in one study. Recommendation there: carbohydrates = remaining calories, ≥ 3-5 g/kg/day "if possible"; off-season bodybuilders report about 5.3 g/kg/day. [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710)
- Henselmans (author's own summary, pointer only): highest glycogen depletion reported for traditional strength training is 39 %. [Author summary](https://mennohenselmans.com/how-many-carbs-for-strength-muscle/)
- REDs: low carbohydrate availability is named in the 2023 IOC consensus as an additional contributor next to low energy availability. [2023 IOC consensus summary](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)

### Inferences

- Numbers a calculation can use (soft checks, not hard limits). Daily carbohydrate target by training load: little or no training: no minimum beyond preference (reference 130 g/day; DGE/EFSA population ranges 45 to 60 % of energy are met by the default presets anyway). Resistance training 3 to 5 sessions per week: about 3 g/kg desirable (Iraki ≥ 3-5), evidence that less hurts is weak (Henselmans). Endurance about 1 h/day: 5-7 g/kg; 1 to 3 h/day: 6-10 g/kg; more than 4 h/day: 8-12 g/kg.
- With the current scheme (protein g/kg, fat 30 %, carbohydrate = rest) a 75 kg person at 2 600 kcal with 1.8 g/kg protein gets about 320 g carbohydrate (4.3 g/kg): fine for strength training and moderate endurance. In a deficit at 1 800 kcal the same person gets about 180 g (2.4 g/kg): below the 3 g/kg soft target, which is where Helms 2014 suggests shifting fat down towards 20 % (still at least 0.5 g/kg) in favour of carbohydrate if training suffers. A sensible rule: when carbohydrates fall below about 3 g/kg for a user who trains, move fat towards its floor before accepting fewer carbohydrates; only show a hint (badge) for endurance users whose carbohydrates are under the tier of their volume.
- A training volume check needs an input the app has: logged training minutes per week. Mapping minutes to the Burke tiers is my inference: under 3 h/week "light" (3-5), 3 to 7 h/week "moderate" (5-7), 7 to 20 h/week "high" (6-10), above "very high" (8-12). The tiers were built for athletes; for recreational users with excess body fat, g/kg of total body weight overstates needs.
- The 130 g RDA should not be enforced as a floor: the "low carb" preset at 20 % of energy gives 75 to 125 g at 1 500 to 2 500 kcal, below 130 g, and the controlled evidence shows no harm for body composition in non athletes. Guard rails there are fiber and saturated fat (DGE conditions).

### Gaps

- Burke 2011 and ACSM 2016 tables not read in the original (confirmed via the DGE sports position that reproduces them, and textbook reproductions).
- The derivation of the 130 g RDA (brain glucose utilisation) was not re-read in the IOM text this session; the number itself is verified.
- DGE evidence-based carbohydrate guideline 2011 (evidence grades: sugar sweetened beverages raise obesity and type 2 diabetes risk, whole grain protective) is cited only through the DGE position paper of 2011, not from the guideline itself.
- No source gives a validated mapping from weekly training hours of recreational users to g/kg carbohydrate; the mapping above is an inference.
- Slater & Phillips 2011 exact wording (4-7 g/kg) verified only via citing papers.

## 4. Fiber, sugar, saturated fat, salt, omega 3, alcohol

### Takeaway

The app's current side targets match German and WHO guidance: fiber at least 30 g/day (DGE; 14.6 g per 1 000 kcal) with 25 g as the EFSA/WHO minimum, free (not total) sugars under 10 % of energy, saturated fat under 10 %, salt under 6 g (DGE) or 5 g (WHO); alcohol supplies 7 kcal/g and since 2024 the DGE names no safe amount, so a tracker should count its calories but set no "target".

### Cited Findings

- Fiber, DGE: guideline value at least 30 g/day for adults, confirmed in the 2021 revision; equals a fiber density of at least 14.6 g per 1 000 kcal (3.5 g/MJ), which is also applied to children and adolescents; it is a guideline value, no physiological requirement exists; actual intake in Germany about 18 g (women) and 19 g (men). [DGE FAQ Ballaststoffe 2021 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/gesunde-ernaehrung/faq/DGE-FAQ-Ballaststoffe-2021.pdf); [report on the 2021 confirmation](https://www.pta-in-love.de/ballaststoffe-dge-bestaetigt-referenzwert-von-30-gramm-pro-tag)
- Fiber, EFSA 2010: 25 g/day adequate for normal laxation in adults; 2 g/MJ for children. [EFSA opinion record](https://zendy.io/title/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1462)
- Fiber, IOM 2005: AI 38 g/day men and 25 g/day women (19 to 50), based on 14 g per 1 000 kcal. [Health Canada DRI tables](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/dietary-reference-intakes/tables/reference-values-macronutrients-dietary-reference-intakes-tables-2005.html)
- Reynolds A, Mann J, Cummings J, et al. "Carbohydrate quality and human health: a series of systematic reviews and meta-analyses." Lancet 2019;393(10170):434-445, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9 (commissioned by WHO): 185 prospective studies (just under 135 million person-years) and 58 clinical trials (4 635 adults); greatest benefit at 25 to 29 g fiber/day, dose response suggests more is better; per 8 g/day more fiber: all cause mortality -7 %, colorectal cancer -8 %, type 2 diabetes -15 %, coronary heart disease -19 %; GRADE certainty moderate for fiber, low to moderate for whole grains, low to very low for glycaemic index/load. [PubMed 30638909](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/); [TCTMD summary with the per 8 g figures](https://www.tctmd.com/news/dose-response-benefits-fiber-intake-seen-across-wide-swath-acquired-diseases)
- DGE 2011: a fiber intake at the reference value cannot be reached with restricted carbohydrate intake and little cereal products; low carbohydrate diets can reach adequate fiber through vegetables, fruit and pulses, but then little of it is cereal fiber. [DGE-Position 2011 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/positionen/DGE-Positionspapier-Richtwerte-Energiezufuhr-KH-und-Fett.pdf)
- Sugar, WHO 2015: free sugars below 10 % of total energy (strong recommendation), further reduction below 5 % (conditional recommendation). Free sugars = sugars added by manufacturer, cook or consumer plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and concentrates; sugars in intact fruit, vegetables and milk are not covered. [News-Medical on the 2015 guideline](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20150305/New-WHO-guidelines-advise-lowering-sugar-intake.aspx); [Consultant360 summary](https://www.consultant360.com/exclusives/new-who-guideline-recommends-cutting-down-free-sugars)
- Sugar, Germany: Ernst JB, Arens-Azevêdo U, Bitzer B, et al. "Quantitative Empfehlung zur Zuckerzufuhr in Deutschland", consensus paper of DAG, DDG and DGE, Bonn 2018 (short version Ernährungs Umschau 2019): free sugars at most 10 % of total energy, following WHO 2015, for children, adolescents and adults; intake in Germany is clearly above that in all groups, most in the young. [Ernährungs Umschau, English short version](https://www.ernaehrungs-umschau.de/english-articles/12-03-2019-quantitative-recommendation-on-sugar-intake-in-germany); [Konsensuspapier (PDF)](https://webarchiv.it.ls.tum.de/kem.wzw.tum.de/fileadmin/pdf/Konsensuspapier_Zucker_DAG_DDG_DGE_2018.pdf)
- EFSA 2010 found insufficient data for an upper limit for added sugars; IOM 2005 suggested at most 25 % of energy from added sugars (an old, much looser bound). [EFSA opinion record](https://zendy.io/title/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1462); [Health Canada DRI tables](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/dietary-reference-intakes/tables/reference-values-macronutrients-dietary-reference-intakes-tables-2005.html)
- Saturated fat: Hooper L et al. "Reduction in saturated fat intake for cardiovascular disease." Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020, CD011737 (RCTs of at least 24 months): reducing saturated fat lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17 % (the earlier version gives 95 % CI 4 to 28 %), with little or no effect on all cause or cardiovascular mortality; benefit seen where SFA were replaced by polyunsaturated fat, not clearly with carbohydrate or protein as replacement (2015 version); data on MUFA replacement very limited. [Cochrane CD011737](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub2/information); [AAFP summary of the 2020 review](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2021/0900/p235.html)
- Saturated fat, guidance: WHO 2023 at most 10 % of energy (trans at most 1 %); DGE at most 10 En% (7 to 10 En% in the DGE sports position); DGE fat guideline 2015: SFA raise LDL cholesterol (convincing), replacing SFA by PUFA lowers CHD risk (probable). [WHO news release 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-07-2023-who-updates-guidelines-on-fats-and-carbohydrates); [DGE Leitlinie Fett 2015, Zusammenfassung (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/leitlinien/fette/12-Zusammenfassung-DGE-Leitlinie-Fett-2015.pdf)
- Salt: DGE orientation value up to 6 g table salt per day (statement 2016); about 70 % of women and 80 % of men in Germany are above it, 39 % and 50 % above 10 g/day. WHO: at most 5 g salt per day for adults. [DGE Pressemeldung 03/2016 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/presse/meldungen/2011-2018/DGE-Pressemeldung-aktuell-03-2016-Speisesalz_01.pdf); [WHO Europe 2022](https://www.who.int/europe/de/news/item/14-03-2022-5-recommendations-to-reduce-salt-intake-to-live-longer-and-healthier-lives)
- Omega 3: EFSA AI EPA+DHA 250 mg/day for adults, plus 100 to 200 mg DHA in pregnancy and lactation; ALA 0.5 E%. DGE sports position: conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA is limited (about 10 to 20 %); fatty fish once or twice a week; EFSA regards supplemental EPA+DHA up to 5 g/day as safe. [EU Knowledge4Policy summary](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/health-promotion-knowledge-gateway/dietary-fats-table-4_en); [Dtsch Z Sportmed 2020](https://www.germanjournalsportsmedicine.com/archive/archive-2020/issue-7-8-9/fats-fat-loading-and-sports-performance-position-of-the-working-group-sports-nutrition-of-the-german-nutrition-society-dge/)
- Alcohol: DGE position paper "Alkohol: Zufuhr in Deutschland, gesundheitliche sowie soziale Folgen und Ableitung von Handlungsempfehlungen" (Richter M, Tauer J, Conrad J, et al.; Ernährungs Umschau 10/2024, doi:10.4455/eu.2024.033; press release August 2024): there is no potentially health promoting and safe amount of alcohol; the DGE recommends not drinking alcohol; the paper replaces the former DGE reference value for alcohol (the 2025 reference value edition lists "Alkohol: DGE-Positionspapier 2024" instead of a chapter). [Ernährungs Umschau Sonderdruck (PDF)](https://ernaehrungs-umschau.de/fileadmin/Ernaehrungs-Umschau/pdfs/pdf_2024/10_24/Sonderdruck_DGE_2_gesamt.pdf); [Ärzteblatt report](https://www.aerzteblatt.de/themen/alkohol/auch-geringe-mengen-alkohol-erhoehen-erkrankungsrisiken-7019b0d2-91c3-40bf-9a1d-34d809087617); [DGE Erläuterungen 2025 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/referenzwerte/DGE_Referenzwerte_Erlaeuterungen_zur_3._Auflage_2025.pdf)
- Alcohol energy: ethanol 29 kJ/g = 7 kcal/g (EU Regulation 1169/2011, Annex XIV). [LMIV Anhang XIV](https://gesetze.legal/eu/vo_eu_2011_1169/anhang_xiv)
- DGE food based dietary guidelines, March 2024 ("Gut essen und trinken", new Ernährungskreis; Schäfer AC et al., Ernährungs Umschau 2024;71(3):M158-M166): derived with a mathematical optimisation model that includes health and environment; plant based, at most 300 g meat and sausage per week, one egg, fish once or twice a week, five portions of fruit and vegetables; valid for healthy adults 18 to 65. These are food based: they do not change the nutrient guideline values (fat 30 %, carbohydrates > 50 %, fiber 30 g). [DGE Interpretationshilfe FBDG (PDF)](https://dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/publikationen/Interpretationshilfe_DGE_FBDG_DEundENG.pdf); [Ökotest report](https://www.oekotest.de/essen-trinken/Mehr-Huelsenfruechte-weniger-Fleisch-DGE-stellt-neue-Empfehlungen-vor-_14483_1.html)

### Inferences

- Fiber target: 30 g/day fixed is the German default; for low calorie days a density target is fairer: max(25 g, 14.6 g × kcal/1 000) would keep 25 g (EFSA/WHO minimum) as the floor and scale up with energy. At 2 000 kcal that gives 29 g, at 3 000 kcal 44 g. Whether the app scales or stays at 30 g is a design choice; both are defensible.
- Sugar: the 10 % limit applies to free sugars. Food databases (BLS, Open Food Facts, EU labels) carry total sugars, which include lactose in milk and sugars in whole fruit. Checking total sugar against 10 % of energy flags people for fruit and dairy, which no guideline intends. Options: label the target as an approximation, or exclude fruit, vegetables and plain dairy (BLS groups) from the sum. BLS 4.0 may carry components that allow an estimate; that needs a check in the data, not in the literature.
- Saturated fat under 10 % is consensus among authorities (WHO 2023, DGE), moderately supported by RCT evidence for events (17 % fewer cardiovascular events, no mortality effect), and contested in the low carb community. Reasonable for an app: keep 10 % as a target shown in orange or red, not as a hard rule; it matters most in the "low carb" preset.
- Salt: 6 g matches the DGE; WHO is stricter at 5 g. Tracking salt from a diary systematically underestimates (salt added in cooking is rarely logged), so the value is a lower bound of real intake.
- Alcohol in a tracker: count 7 kcal/g towards the day's energy. It belongs to neither fat nor carbohydrate; if the app shows macro shares from 4/4/9 only, alcoholic drinks leave a gap between the kcal number and the macro sum, which is correct and should not be "filled" by booking alcohol as carbohydrate or fat. No target or allowance for alcohol (DGE 2024: none is safe); a neutral display of grams and kcal fits the position.
- Omega 3 (250 mg EPA+DHA) cannot be tracked reliably from product labels (not a mandatory label value); BLS foods carry fatty acids, OFF products mostly do not. A weekly rather than daily view fits the "fish once or twice a week" guidance.

### Gaps

- Number of trials and participants in Hooper 2020 not verified (commonly cited as 15 RCTs with about 59 000 participants; the search could not confirm it). The 95 % CI 4 to 28 % is from the plain language summary of the earlier version.
- WHO 2015 sugar guideline, WHO sodium guideline 2012 (2 g sodium = 5 g salt) and the WHO 2023 guideline PDFs were not opened; numbers confirmed via WHO news pages and secondary reports.
- A "15 to 30 % risk reduction" for the highest vs lowest fiber consumers (Reynolds 2019) is widely quoted, but this session confirmed only the per 8 g figures and the 25 to 29 g optimum.
- The 50 g/day figure often quoted for free sugars (10 % of 2 000 kcal) was not found in the consensus paper snippets; it is simple arithmetic, not a separate recommendation.
- I did not check whether the DGE or EFSA revised the EPA+DHA value after 2010; the DGE/ÖGE 2025 edition lists "Essenzielle Fettsäuren" with derivation state 2000.
- EFSA's 2022 opinion on a tolerable upper intake level for dietary sugars (conclusion as I recall it: no UL could be set, intake of added and free sugars should be as low as possible) was not verified this session.

## 5. Thermic effect, satiety and energy density of fat vs carbohydrate

### Takeaway

Carbohydrate has a slightly higher thermic effect than fat (5-10 % vs 0-3 % of its energy), but the net difference at equal calories is tens of kcal per day and does not justify different calorie targets; for satiety, energy density and food quality matter more than the macro ratio, and the direct comparison (Hall 2021) favoured the low fat, low energy density diet for spontaneous intake.

### Cited Findings

- Westerterp KR. "Diet induced thermogenesis." Nutr Metab (Lond) 2004;1:5 (review, verified from PMC): reported thermic effect by nutrient: fat 0-3 %, carbohydrate 5-10 %, protein 20-30 %, alcohol 10-30 % of the energy content; mixed diet about 10 % of energy intake (abstract: 5 to 15 % of daily energy expenditure); main determinants are energy content and the protein and alcohol fractions. [PMC524030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC524030)
- Controlled feeding at equal calories and protein: 26 kcal/day higher expenditure on lower fat diets (Hall & Guo 2017), which is the size one would expect from the thermic effect difference. [PMC5568065](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568065)
- Ad libitum intake: 689 ± 73 kcal/day less on a low fat plant based diet than on a ketogenic animal based diet over 2 weeks (n = 20), with equal ratings of hunger, fullness and enjoyment; the ketogenic diet had about twice the energy density (kcal per gram of food). The authors note this contradicts the carbohydrate-insulin model but does not fully support a pure passive overconsumption model either. [Paper record](https://oar.a-star.edu.sg/communities-collections/articles/16959); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20210309/lower-energy-intake-observed-in-lowfat-vegan-vs-lowcarb-ketogenic-diet)
- DGE: the fat guideline value aims, among other things, at lower energy intake and energy density and thereby lower obesity risk; with uncontrolled energy intake a higher fat share raises obesity risk with probable evidence (fat guideline 2015). [DGE-Position 2011 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/positionen/DGE-Positionspapier-Richtwerte-Energiezufuhr-KH-und-Fett.pdf); [DGE Leitlinie Fett 2015, Zusammenfassung (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/wissenschaft/leitlinien/fette/12-Zusammenfassung-DGE-Leitlinie-Fett-2015.pdf)
- DGE sports position names satiety between meals as one reason not to go below 20 E% fat. [Dtsch Z Sportmed 2020](https://www.germanjournalsportsmedicine.com/archive/archive-2020/issue-7-8-9/fats-fat-loading-and-sports-performance-position-of-the-working-group-sports-nutrition-of-the-german-nutrition-society-dge/)
- DIETFITS: in free living conditions with counselling on food quality in both arms, low fat and low carbohydrate produced the same weight loss over 12 months. [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180220123124.htm)

### Inferences

- Size of the thermic effect difference: moving 20 % of a 2 500 kcal diet (500 kcal) from fat to carbohydrate changes the thermic effect by roughly 500 × (0.075 - 0.015) ≈ 30 kcal/day (midpoints of Westerterp's ranges). This is below the resolution of any calorie tracker; the app should not adjust calorie targets by macro split. Protein is the only macro whose thermic effect (20-30 %) is large enough to discuss, and that belongs to the protein researcher.
- Satiety guidance for target setting: no evidence based reason to prefer a fat or carbohydrate share for hunger control once protein is set. People differ; the practical lever is energy density and fiber (vegetables, pulses, whole grain), which the fiber target already rewards. Short term trials favour low energy density (typically lower fat), long term free living trials show no difference: a draw for presets.
- The "diet" preset lowering fat to 25 % is consistent with this: fat has 9 kcal/g, so trimming fat frees the most food volume per kcal while carbohydrates stay available for training. It is a reasonable default, not a proven optimum.

### Gaps

- No dedicated meta-analysis on satiety of fat vs carbohydrate at matched protein and energy density was retrieved; classic work on energy density (Rolls) and the satiety index (Holt 1995) was not verified this session and is therefore not cited.
- Hall 2021 is a 2 week inpatient study with n = 20; it does not show long term effects.

## 6. Atwater factors, fiber at 2 kcal/g, polyols

### Takeaway

4/4/9 (plus 7 for alcohol) is the legal basis of every EU label and accurate enough for target setting; to reproduce label kcal exactly an app must add fiber at 2 kcal/g, polyols at 2.4 kcal/g (erythritol 0) and organic acids at 3 kcal/g, because EU "carbohydrate" excludes fiber.

### Cited Findings

- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex XIV, conversion factors (verified): carbohydrate (except polyols) 17 kJ/g = 4 kcal/g; polyols 10 kJ/g = 2.4 kcal/g; protein 17 kJ/g = 4 kcal/g; fat 37 kJ/g = 9 kcal/g; salatrims 25 kJ/g = 6 kcal/g; ethanol 29 kJ/g = 7 kcal/g; organic acids 13 kJ/g = 3 kcal/g; fiber 8 kJ/g = 2 kcal/g; erythritol 0 kJ/g = 0 kcal/g. [LMIV Anhang XIV](https://gesetze.legal/eu/vo_eu_2011_1169/anhang_xiv)
- IOM 2005 and the EU differ in convention: the EU declares available carbohydrate without fiber (fiber separately at 2 kcal/g), US labels traditionally include fiber in total carbohydrate. This follows from the Annex XIV table (separate fiber row). [LMIV Anhang XIV](https://gesetze.legal/eu/vo_eu_2011_1169/anhang_xiv)
- DGE fiber guideline as energy density: 14.6 g per 1 000 kcal. [DGE FAQ Ballaststoffe 2021 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/gesunde-ernaehrung/faq/DGE-FAQ-Ballaststoffe-2021.pdf)

### Inferences

- Energy from fiber at the target intake: 30 g × 2 kcal = 60 kcal/day, about 2 to 3 % of energy. A macro split computed from 4/4/9 alone will therefore sum to slightly less than the label kcal for high fiber foods; this explains small gaps between "kcal" and "kcal from macros" and is not an error. The app already uses 4/4/9 + 2 for fiber in its custom food formula, which matches Annex XIV. For macro targets (grams from an energy share) plain 4/4/9 is the right choice, because targets are set on protein, fat and available carbohydrate.
- Polyols: products with sugar alcohols (protein bars, sugar free sweets) list polyols as part of carbohydrate on EU labels ("davon mehrwertige Alkohole") but they count 2.4 kcal/g, erythritol 0. An app that computes kcal as 4 × carbohydrate overstates such products by up to 40 % of the polyol energy (100 % for erythritol). Trusting the label kcal instead of recomputing avoids this; for the carbohydrate target, polyols count as carbohydrate grams by label convention, which slightly overstates glycaemic carbohydrate. This affects few foods and needs no special handling beyond preferring the declared kcal.
- Accuracy overall: Atwater general factors are averages; errors of a few percent per food are small compared with portion estimation and label tolerance. They are accurate enough for an app. Mixed diets rich in nuts, whole grains and pulses deliver somewhat less metabolisable energy than the factors predict, which errs on the safe side for weight loss.

### Gaps

- I did not retrieve a primary source in this session quantifying the error of Atwater general factors (commonly cited: specific factors differ by food, almonds yield about 20 to 30 % less metabolisable energy than calculated, Novotny et al. 2012; EU label tolerances for declared nutrients of about ±20 % from the Commission guidance of 2012). These figures are from background knowledge, unverified here, and should be checked before being stated in a report.
- No source was retrieved on the real metabolisable energy of fiber (fermentable vs non fermentable); 2 kcal/g is a regulatory average.

## 7. Sex and age differences

### Takeaway

Reference ranges for fat and carbohydrate as shares of energy do not differ by sex or adult age; differences arise in grams (IOM AIs for linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid and fiber are lower for women because they scale with energy) and in risk: women with low energy availability, not low fat as such, are the group with documented hormonal consequences.

### Cited Findings

- IOM 2005, adults 19 to 50: fiber AI 38 g men vs 25 g women (both from 14 g per 1 000 kcal); linoleic acid 17 vs 12 g/day; alpha-linolenic acid 1.6 vs 1.1 g/day; AMDRs identical for both sexes. [Health Canada DRI tables](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/dietary-reference-intakes/tables/reference-values-macronutrients-dietary-reference-intakes-tables-2005.html)
- DGE: fiber guideline at least 30 g/day for adults of both sexes, equal to 14.6 g per 1 000 kcal. [DGE FAQ Ballaststoffe 2021 (PDF)](https://www.dge.de/fileadmin/dok/gesunde-ernaehrung/faq/DGE-FAQ-Ballaststoffe-2021.pdf)
- EFSA: additional 100 to 200 mg preformed DHA per day in pregnancy and lactation on top of 250 mg EPA+DHA. [Record of the EFSA fat opinion](https://peeref.com/works/63881402)
- Women, fat intake and hormones (BioCycle, n = 259, observational): higher fat intake associated with about 4 % higher testosterone, no association with estradiol, LH, FSH, SHBG, and total fat not associated with anovulation. [PubMed 26843151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26843151/)
- Men: low fat (vs high fat) diets lowered total testosterone, SMD -0.38 (6 studies, 206 men); stronger in men of European and North American origin (-0.52). [arXiv accepted manuscript](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.00007)
- Energy availability: about 45 kcal/kg FFM/day is considered optimal for women, below 30 kcal/kg FFM/day many body systems are perturbed (IOC 2018, based on Loucks' laboratory work); 30 kcal/kg FFM roughly equals resting metabolic rate; the 2023 consensus stresses that REDs also affects men and adds low carbohydrate availability. [IOC consensus 2018 update (PDF)](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Margo_Mountjoy/publication/325212903_International_Olympic_Committee_IOC_Consensus_Statement_on_Relative_Energy_Deficiency_in_Sport_RED-S_2018_Update/links/5dfe4294299bf10bc36eadd4/International-Olympic-Committee-IOC-Consensus-Statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-RED-S-2018-Update.pdf); [BMJ Group on the 2023 statement](https://bmjgroup.com/prolonged-mismatch-between-calories-eaten-and-burned-may-be-putting-many-athletes-at-risk-of-reds/)
- Helms 2014: in strength trained, normal weight women a loss rate of 1 kg/week reduced testosterone 30 % more than 0.5 kg/week (rate of loss matters for hormones). [PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492)
- DGE food based guidelines 2024 apply to healthy adults aged 18 to 65. [Ökotest report](https://www.oekotest.de/essen-trinken/Mehr-Huelsenfruechte-weniger-Fleisch-DGE-stellt-neue-Empfehlungen-vor-_14483_1.html)

### Inferences

- No sex specific fat or carbohydrate percentages are needed. A g/kg fat floor treats lighter people (often women) fairly: 0.5 g/kg at 55 kg is 28 g, which still covers essential fatty acids by a wide margin (need about 7 to 13 g LA + ALA).
- The more useful protection for women (and lean men) is a floor on the deficit: an energy availability check needs fat free mass and exercise energy, which the app may not know; a simpler proxy is not letting the calorie target fall below resting energy expenditure, or warning at fast loss rates (> 1 % of body weight per week, Helms). That belongs to the calorie researcher but interacts with the fat floor: at very low calories protein and the fat floor leave almost no carbohydrate, which is itself a signal that the target is too low.
- Age: for older adults the references do not change fat or carbohydrate shares; protein and energy change (other researcher). Fiber stays at 30 g or 14.6 g per 1 000 kcal, so a density based target automatically adapts to lower energy needs with age.

### Gaps

- Loucks & Thuma 2003 (LH pulsatility disrupted below about 30 kcal/kg FFM/day) was not retrieved; the threshold is cited via the IOC statement.
- No intervention trial was found that tests low fat (below 20 % of energy) at adequate energy on menstrual function in women; evidence here is observational (Mumford 2016) or confounded by energy deficit.
- Postmenopausal women, pregnancy and lactation (beyond the DHA note) and adults over 65 were not researched in depth; the brief was healthy adults in general.
