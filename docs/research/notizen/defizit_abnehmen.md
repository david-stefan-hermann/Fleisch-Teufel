# Energy deficit for fat loss in healthy adults: rate, deficit size, lower limits, muscle retention

Notes on verification: abstracts were checked through Europe PMC / PubMed unless a line says otherwise. "Secondary source only" means the number could not be read in the primary text. Lines marked "(my arithmetic)" are calculations from cited numbers, not statements of the source. Protein evidence is covered by another researcher and only touched where a deficit study depends on it.

## 1. Rate of loss and lean mass retention: how fast, and should the rate scale with body weight?

### Takeaway

For lean and resistance-trained people the sports nutrition literature converges on about 0.5 to 1 % of body weight per week (0.5 % or less when already lean), based on few and small trials; for people with overweight or obesity, guidelines accept about 0.5 to 1 kg/week, and physiology (Forbes/Hall) says a fatter body loses a smaller share of lean mass at the same deficit. A fixed kg/week menu that ignores body weight is therefore too aggressive for light and lean users and needlessly timid for heavy ones: a percentage of body weight fits the evidence better.

### Cited Findings

- Garthe et al. 2011 (Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 21:97-104), RCT, 24 elite athletes, 4 resistance sessions/week on top of sport training: target 0.7 %/week (slow, n=13, 8.5 ± 2.2 weeks, intake reduced 19 ± 2 %) vs 1.4 %/week (fast, n=11, 5.3 ± 0.9 weeks, intake reduced 30 ± 4 %). Same total loss (5.6 % vs 5.5 % of body weight; actual rates 0.7 vs 1.0 %/week). Fat mass fell 31 ± 3 % vs 21 ± 4 %; lean body mass changed +2.1 ± 0.4 % (slow) vs -0.2 ± 0.7 % (fast), p < .01; strength gains were larger in the slow group. Authors recommend 0.7 %/week for athletes who want to gain lean mass while losing weight: [Garthe 2011, PubMed 21558571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)
- Mero et al. 2010 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 7:4), 15 normal-weight recreationally trained women, 4 weeks, protein at least 1.4 g/kg: deficit 1100 kcal/day (1 kg/week, n=8) vs 550 kcal/day (0.5 kg/week, n=7). Lean mass did not change in either group; serum testosterone fell 30 % vs 3 %; bench press performance fell only in the 1 kg group. Authors recommend 0.5 kg/week: [Mero 2010, PubMed 20205751](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20205751/)
- Helms, Aragon, Fitschen 2014 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:20), narrative review for natural bodybuilders: "Caloric intake should be set at a level that results in bodyweight losses of approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk to maximize muscle retention"; the leaner the competitor, the higher the risk of lean mass loss, so the rate should slow as leanness increases: [Helms 2014, PMC4033492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492)
- Roberts, Helms, Trexler, Fitschen 2020 (J Hum Kinet 71:79-108), review for physique athletes: "slower rates of weight loss (≤0.5% of body mass per week) are preferable for attenuating the loss of fat-free mass" in contest preparation: [Roberts 2020, PubMed 32148575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148575/)
- Ruiz-Castellano et al. 2021 (Nutrients 13:3255), narrative review for resistance-trained athletes: target loss 0.5 to 1.0 % of body weight per week: [Ruiz-Castellano 2021, PubMed 34579132](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34579132/)
- ISSN position stand on diets and body composition (Aragon et al. 2017, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:16): "The higher the baseline body fat level, the more aggressively the caloric deficit may be imposed. Slower rates of weight loss can better preserve lean mass (LM) in leaner subjects.": [Aragon 2017, PubMed 28630601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/)
- Ashtary-Larky et al. 2020 (Br J Nutr), meta-analysis of 7 trials comparing gradual and rapid loss with similar total weight loss: gradual loss reduced fat mass by about 1 kg more (95 % CI -1.70 to -0.29) and preserved resting metabolic rate better (about 407 kJ/day); no significant difference in fat-free mass: [Ashtary-Larky 2020, PubMed 32576318](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32576318/)
- Ashtary-Larky et al. 2017 (Int J Endocrinol Metab 15:e13249), RCT, n=42 with overweight or obesity, at least 5 % loss in 5 weeks vs 15 weeks: slow loss reduced more fat; rapid loss reduced more lean mass, body water and resting metabolic rate: [Ashtary-Larky 2017, PubMed 29201070](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29201070/)
- Forbes relation as restated by Hall 2007 (Br J Nutr 97:1059-1063): cross-sectional FFM = 10.4 ln(FM) + 14.2 (kg, women's data), so for small changes the lean share of a weight change is 10.4 / (10.4 + FM). Hall's extension for large changes shows that Forbes' original formula underestimates lean loss when the loss is large; in both versions more initial fat means a smaller lean share. The model does not include exercise, protein or rate: [Hall 2007, PubMed 17367567](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17367567/)
- Hall 2008 (Int J Obes 32:573-576): the energy content of lost weight is 3500 kcal/lb (32.2 MJ/kg, about 7700 kcal/kg) only for people with more than about 30 kg of initial body fat; for leaner people a kg of weight loss holds less energy, because more of it is lean tissue. The same model explains why men lose more weight than women at the same deficit: [Hall 2008, PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- Dulloo and Jacquet 1999 (Br J Nutr 82:339-356), reanalysis of the Minnesota starvation experiment: the higher the initial percentage of body fat, the lower the share of mobilised energy that came from protein: [Dulloo 1999, PubMed 10673906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10673906/)
- Heymsfield et al. 2014 (Obes Rev 15:310-321): the "one quarter of weight loss is fat-free mass" rule is an oversimplification; the lean share varies with energy intake level, diet composition, sex, baseline adiposity, inactivity or exercise, and metabolic state: [Heymsfield 2014, PubMed 24447775](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24447775/)
- Chaston, Dixon, O'Brien 2007 (Int J Obes 31:743-750), systematic review: the degree of caloric restriction was positively associated with the percentage of weight lost as fat-free mass (r² = 0.31, p = 0.006); exercise lowered it: [Chaston 2007, PubMed 17075583](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17075583/)
- Alpert 2005 (J Theor Biol 233:1-13), theoretical model fitted to underfeeding data: fat stores can transfer at most (290 ± 25) kJ per kg of fat per day to the fat-free mass; a larger deficit must be covered from fat-free mass. That is about 69 kcal per kg of fat per day (my arithmetic). Single-author model, derived from moderately active underfed subjects, not tested prospectively: [Alpert 2005, PubMed 15615615](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15615615/)
- German S3 guideline "Prävention und Therapie der Adipositas" (AWMF 050-001, version 5.0, October 2024), recommendation 5.11 (expert consensus, 94 %): a daily energy deficit of about 500 to 600 kcal, or an individually determined deficit; background text: about 0.5 kg/week can be expected for at most three months, the loss then slows and a new energy balance is reached after 3 to 6 months; about 5 to 6 kg after 12 months. A larger deficit is possible at higher BMI. Recommendation 5.3: goals within 6 to 12 months are at least 5 % of initial weight at BMI 25 to 34.9 and at least 10 % at BMI 35 and above: [AWMF S3 Adipositas 2024 (PDF)](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/050-001l_S3_Praevention-Therapie-Adipositas_2024-10.pdf)
- 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline (Jensen et al., Circulation): recommends sustained loss of 5 to 10 % of body weight within the first six months; even 3 % sustained loss lowers diabetes risk: [ACC press release 2013](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2013/11/12/15/20/111213acc_aha_obesity_guideline); guideline: [doi:10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee)
- NHLBI Clinical Guidelines 1998 as summarised by the American Family Physician: deficit of 500 to 1,000 kcal/day for a loss of 1 to 2 lb (about 0.45 to 0.9 kg) per week, aiming at 10 % in six months: [AAFP 2000 summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2000/0615/p3615)
- ACSM position stand 2001 (Jakicic et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 33:2145-2156): "An energy deficit of 500-1000 kcal.d-1 achieved through reductions in total energy intake is recommended": [ACSM 2001, PubMed 11740312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11740312/)
- Finkler, Heymsfield, St-Onge 2012 (J Acad Nutr Diet 112:75-80): in published weight-loss studies the prescribed 0.5 to 1 kg/week is rarely achieved; the realised rate falls with study length and is higher in older and heavier subjects: [Finkler 2012, PubMed 22717178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22717178/)

### Inferences

- Consensus (moderate evidence, mostly reviews built on two small RCTs): lean or training users should lose about 0.5 to 1 % of body weight per week; near the lean end 0.5 % or less. Consensus (guidelines, strong for health outcomes, not for body composition): people with overweight or obesity can lose 0.5 to 1 kg/week, which at 100 kg is also 0.5 to 1 %/week. Both camps meet at roughly 0.5 to 1 %/week, so one percentage scale can serve all users.
- What the current menu means in percent (my arithmetic): 1 kg/week is 1.7 %/week at 60 kg, 1.25 % at 80 kg, 0.8 % at 120 kg. 0.5 kg/week is 0.83 % at 60 kg and 0.42 % at 120 kg. For a 60 kg user every option from 0.75 kg/week up exceeds the 1 %/week upper bound of the sports literature.
- Usable numbers for a calculation: default 0.5 %/week; "slow" 0.25 %; "fast" 0.75 %; hard cap 1 %/week. A leanness modifier is defensible (cap 0.5 %/week for BMI below about 23, or for users who say they are lean and train), following Helms, Roberts and the ISSN stand.
- Forbes examples (my arithmetic from 10.4 / (10.4 + FM), small changes, no training): 10 kg fat gives about 51 % lean share, 20 kg about 34 %, 30 kg about 26 %, 50 kg about 17 %. These are untrained cross-sectional values; resistance training and protein lower them a lot (section 2), so they show direction, not a forecast.
- Alpert's limit as a plausibility check (my arithmetic): 69 kcal per kg of fat per day allows about 690 kcal/day at 10 kg fat, 1380 kcal/day at 20 kg, 2070 kcal/day at 30 kg. A 70 kg man with 12 % fat (8.4 kg) would be limited to about 580 kcal/day, which is close to 0.5 kg/week or 0.75 %/week. The model agrees in direction with the percentage rules but is not validated; do not present it as fact.
- Contested: whether slow loss protects lean mass in people with obesity. The 2020 meta-analysis found more fat loss and better RMR with gradual loss but no significant lean mass difference; Garthe is the only RCT in athletes and the follow-up reportedly showed no group difference after 12 months (secondary pointer, not verified).

### Gaps

- NICE: the "600 kcal/day deficit" of the older CG189 could not be confirmed in the current NG246 (published 14 January 2025, replaces CG189; page returned 403). Treat the 600 kcal figure as a historical NICE number until checked: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246
- No peer-reviewed critique or experimental test of Alpert's 290 kJ/kg limit was found; critiques exist in blogs only. Its own abstract notes a disagreement with other observers on RMR.
- The confidence interval of the RMR effect in Ashtary-Larky 2020 looked inconsistent in the retrieved text and is not quoted.
- No trial compares percent-based against fixed kg/week targets directly; the percent scaling is an inference from Forbes/Hall plus the athlete literature.
- The Garthe 12-month follow-up (no lasting body composition difference) was seen only as a search result, not verified.

## 2. Size of the deficit: percent of maintenance, the 500 kcal rule, and what resistance training changes

### Takeaway

A moderate deficit of about 500 to 600 kcal/day or roughly 20 to 25 % of maintenance is the common ground of guidelines and trials; larger deficits (30 to 40 %) raise the lean share of the loss unless protein is high and training is hard. Resistance training is the strongest single lever for keeping lean mass in a deficit, but a deficit of about 500 kcal/day is already enough to stop lean mass gains.

### Cited Findings

- Murphy and Koehler 2022 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 32:125-137), meta-analysis of resistance training in an energy deficit of at least 3 weeks: lean mass gains were impaired (effect size -0.57, p = 0.02), strength gains were not (ES -0.31, p = 0.28); in the second analysis lean mass ES was -0.11 with a deficit vs +0.20 without; meta-regression: a deficit of about 500 kcal/day was the point at which lean mass gains were prevented: [Murphy 2022, PubMed 34623696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)
- Longland et al. 2016 (Am J Clin Nutr 103:738-746), 40 young overweight men, 4 weeks, about 40 % deficit, resistance and interval training 6 days/week: protein 2.4 g/kg gave +1.2 kg lean mass and -4.8 kg fat; 1.2 g/kg gave +0.1 kg lean mass and -3.5 kg fat. A large deficit can spare lean mass in untrained, overweight beginners under heavy training and high protein: [Longland 2016, PubMed 26817506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)
- Garthe 2011: 19 % intake reduction gained lean mass, 30 % reduction did not (athletes, 4 strength sessions/week): [Garthe 2011, PubMed 21558571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)
- MATADOR used 67 % of maintenance (33 % deficit) in men with obesity and saw only 1.2 to 1.8 kg fat-free mass loss alongside 8 to 12 kg fat loss: [Byrne 2018, PubMed 28925405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/)
- CALERIE phase 1 (Redman et al. 2009, PLoS One 4:e4377), overweight adults, 6 months: 25 % restriction gave -10.4 % body weight; 12.5 % restriction plus 12.5 % exercise gave -10.0 % and, unlike diet alone, no metabolic adaptation and no drop in activity: [Redman 2009, PubMed 19198647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19198647/)
- Lopez et al. 2022 (Obes Rev 23:e13428), meta-analysis, 114 trials, n = 4184 with overweight or obesity: resistance training plus caloric restriction reduced fat mass by 5.3 kg while lean mass was maintained: [Lopez 2022, PubMed 35191588](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35191588/)
- Sardeli et al. 2018 (Nutrients 10:423), meta-analysis of 6 RCTs in older adults with obesity: resistance training prevented 93.5 % of the lean mass loss caused by caloric restriction (0.819 kg, 95 % CI 0.364 to 1.273): [Sardeli 2018, PubMed 29596307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29596307/)
- Clark 2015 (J Diabetes Metab Disord 14:31), meta-analysis of 66 studies: diet combined with resistance training was the most effective combination for body composition; diet alone the least for retaining fat-free mass: [Clark 2015, PubMed 25973403](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25973403/)
- Cava, Yeat, Mittendorfer 2017 (Adv Nutr 8:511-519), review: weight loss by diet reduces muscle mass without improving strength per se; resistance and endurance exercise help preserve muscle during weight loss: [Cava 2017, PubMed 28507015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28507015/)
- Weinheimer, Sands, Campbell 2010 (Nutr Rev 68:375-388), systematic review, adults 50 and older: 81 % of diet-only groups but 39 % of diet-plus-exercise groups lost 15 % or more of their weight loss as fat-free mass: [Weinheimer 2010, PubMed 20591106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20591106/)
- Conflict: the ACSM stand of 2001 said resistance exercise "will increase strength and function but may not attenuate the loss of fat-free mass": [ACSM 2001, PubMed 11740312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11740312/); contradicted by the later meta-analyses [Lopez 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35191588/) and [Sardeli 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29596307/)
- German S3 guideline 2024: 500 to 600 kcal/day deficit as the standard; larger at higher BMI: [AWMF S3 Adipositas 2024 (PDF)](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/050-001l_S3_Praevention-Therapie-Adipositas_2024-10.pdf)
- The static "500 kcal/day equals 1 lb/week" rule overestimates the loss: Thomas et al. 2013 tested the 3500 kcal rule against seven controlled feeding experiments and found it grossly overpredicts: [Thomas 2013, PubMed 23628852](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23628852/)

### Inferences

- Consensus: 500 to 600 kcal/day (guidelines) and 20 to 25 % of maintenance (CALERIE, refeed and diet-break trials all used 25 %) describe the same zone for a person with 2000 to 2800 kcal expenditure. Percent of maintenance scales by itself with body size and activity, which a fixed kcal number does not.
- Usable numbers: standard deficit 20 % of estimated expenditure; upper limit 25 % for lean or training users, 30 to 35 % only for users with clearly elevated body fat (MATADOR 33 %, Garthe fast arm 30 %). 40 % was studied for 4 weeks under supervision only.
- Cross-check of rate and deficit (my arithmetic at 7700 kcal/kg): 0.5 %/week at 80 kg is 0.4 kg/week or 440 kcal/day; 1 %/week is 880 kcal/day. With 2500 kcal expenditure these are 18 % and 35 %. A rule "the smaller of 1 %/week and 25 to 30 % of expenditure" keeps both views consistent.
- The app could tell users who log strength training that a deficit above about 500 kcal/day makes muscle gain unlikely (Murphy and Koehler), while muscle retention remains realistic. The 500 kcal threshold itself is contested: the analysis behind it rests largely on trials in older adults and a regression over few studies (the critique was found only in a blog, so the weakness is noted here without a citable source).
- Resistance training evidence is strong and consistent (several meta-analyses); the old ACSM doubt is outdated.

### Gaps

- No dose-response meta-analysis of deficit size in percent of maintenance against lean mass loss in young, trained adults was found; Chaston 2007 covers obesity treatments including VLCD and surgery.
- Exact wording of the AHA/ACC/TOS 2013 calorie prescriptions could not be read in the primary text (see section 3).
- Protein thresholds that interact with deficit size are left to the protein researcher.

## 3. Lower limits: where 1200/1500 kcal comes from, VLCD, micronutrients, energy availability

### Takeaway

The 1200 (women) and 1500 (men) kcal floors are conventions taken from the low-calorie-diet ranges of US obesity guidelines, backed by the practical difficulty of covering micronutrients below about 1200 kcal, not by an outcome trial; they fit short or small people poorly and tall or active people worse. The physiologically founded limit is energy availability: below about 30 kcal per kg fat-free mass per day hormonal disruption begins in lean women, and for lean men about 25 kcal/kg FFM is proposed, while intakes under 800 to 1200 kcal belong to supervised medical programmes.

### Cited Findings

- 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline: prescribes 1,200 to 1,500 kcal/day for women and 1,500 to 1,800 kcal/day for men (adjusted for body weight), or a 500 or 750 kcal/day deficit; diets under 800 kcal/day only with medical supervision. Verified through secondary reproductions only; the press release itself speaks of "a moderately reduced calorie diet": guideline [doi:10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee); [ACC press release](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2013/11/12/15/20/111213acc_aha_obesity_guideline)
- NHLBI 1998 as summarised by AAFP: low-calorie diets of 1,000 to 1,200 kcal/day for most women and 1,200 to 1,500 kcal/day for men (and heavier or more active women): [AAFP 2000 summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2000/0615/p3615)
- National Task Force on the Prevention and Treatment of Obesity 1993 (JAMA 270:967-974): VLCD of 800 kcal/day or less produce 1.5 to 2.5 kg/week and about 20 kg in 12 to 16 weeks vs 0.4 to 0.5 kg/week on a standard 1200 kcal diet; they need at least 1 g protein per kg ideal body weight and medical supervision; gallstones are the most common complication; long-term maintenance is no better: [National Task Force 1993, PubMed 8345648](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8345648/)
- German S3 guideline 2024, recommendation 5.14: formula diets of 800 to 1200 kcal/day, usually no longer than 12 weeks, with medical supervision; they produce about 1.0 to 2.5 kg/week: [AWMF S3 Adipositas 2024 (PDF)](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/050-001l_S3_Praevention-Therapie-Adipositas_2024-10.pdf)
- NICE NG246 (2025): low-energy diets (800 to 1,200 kcal/day) and very-low-energy diets (under 800 kcal/day) only as part of a multicomponent strategy with specialist support, nutritionally complete, for no more than 12 weeks: [NICE NG246](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246)
- Micronutrients: Gardner et al. 2010 (Am J Clin Nutr 92:304-312), A TO Z trial in women: after 8 weeks on four popular weight-loss diets the share of participants at risk of inadequate intake rose for several micronutrients, differing by diet: [Gardner 2010, PubMed 20573800](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20573800/)
- Calton 2010 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 7:24): suggested menus of four popular diet plans (mean 1748 kcal) covered on average 11.75 of 27 micronutrients at 100 % of the US reference intake. Menu analysis by a single author with a commercial interest in supplements, weak evidence: [Calton 2010, PubMed 20537171](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20537171/)
- Engel et al. 2018 (Nutrients 10:108): three commercial plans analysed with a nutrient tracker each missed 90 % of the reference intake for several micronutrients (vitamin D, calcium, others), some even after scaling to 2000 kcal: [Engel 2018, PubMed 29361684](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29361684/)
- Loucks and Thuma 2003 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 88:297-311), 29 sedentary, regularly menstruating young women, 5 days at energy availability 10, 20 or 30 vs 45 kcal/kg lean body mass/day: LH pulsatility was unaffected at 30 but disrupted below it; other hormones (insulin, T3, IGF-I, leptin) responded along different curves: [Loucks 2003, PubMed 12519869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/)
- Loucks, Kiens, Wright 2011 (J Sports Sci 29 Suppl 1:S7-15): energy availability = intake minus exercise energy expenditure, per kg fat-free mass; about 45 kcal/kg FFM/day is balance for young adults, 30 the level below which reproductive and bone markers suffer; for weight loss they advise staying at 30 to 45: [Loucks 2011, PubMed 21793767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21793767/)
- IOC consensus update 2018 on RED-S (Mountjoy et al., Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 28:316-331): low energy availability impairs many body systems in both sexes; the 30 kcal/kg FFM threshold comes from short laboratory studies in women and does not predict outcomes for every individual: [Mountjoy 2018, PubMed 29771168](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29771168/)
- IOC consensus 2023 on REDs (Mountjoy et al., Br J Sports Med 57:1073-1097): describes low energy availability as a continuum from adaptable to problematic exposure; duration, severity and moderating factors decide the outcome: [Mountjoy 2023, PubMed 37752011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)
- Men: Fagerberg 2018 (Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 28:385-402), review of male bodybuilders: prolonged energy availability below 25 kcal/kg FFM brings muscle loss, hormonal disturbance and psychological problems near 4 to 5 % body fat; an EA above 25 is suggested to limit them: [Fagerberg 2018, PubMed 28530498](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28530498/)
- Men: Koehler et al. 2016 (J Sports Sci 34:1921-1929), 6 exercising men, 4 days at 15 vs 40 kcal/kg FFM: leptin -53 to -56 %, insulin -34 to -38 %, no significant change in testosterone, T3, IGF-1 or ghrelin: [Koehler 2016, PubMed 26852783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26852783/)
- Torstveit et al. 2018: male endurance athletes with larger within-day energy deficits had suppressed resting metabolic rate and less favourable hormones: [Torstveit 2018, PubMed 29405793](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29405793/)
- Underweight: in the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (Lancet 2016, 239 prospective studies, never-smokers without chronic disease) mortality was lowest at BMI 20 to 25; hazard ratio 1.13 (1.09 to 1.17) at BMI 18.5 to under 20 and 1.51 (1.43 to 1.59) at BMI 15 to under 18.5: [Global BMI Mortality Collaboration 2016, PubMed 27423262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27423262/)

### Inferences

- Origin of the floors: the app's 1200/1500 are the lower ends of the women's and men's ranges in the US guidelines (NHLBI 1998, AHA/ACC/TOS 2013), which in turn mark the border between "low-calorie diet" and the supervised 800 to 1200 kcal zone that the German guideline and NICE reserve for formula diets under medical care. That is a sound reason for an unsupervised consumer app to stop there, but it is convention plus micronutrient practicality, not a tested threshold.
- Sex is a proxy for body size here. The US guideline itself says "adjusted for body weight". A floor that scales would be more consistent than two fixed numbers.
- Energy availability as a scalable floor (my arithmetic): woman 60 kg with 25 % fat, FFM 45 kg: 30 kcal/kg FFM is 1350 kcal plus what she burns in training. Man 80 kg with 18 % fat, FFM 65.6 kg: 30 gives about 1970 kcal, 25 gives about 1640 kcal, each plus exercise. So for lean, active men 1500 kcal is below the level the sports literature considers safe, and for a small woman 1200 kcal is roughly at the 30 kcal/kg line only if she does not train.
- Scope warning: the 30 kcal/kg FFM line comes from lean women and athletes. People with obesity routinely and safely go far below it in supervised programmes (their fat stores buffer the deficit, see Forbes/Alpert in section 1). Applying it to everyone would forbid guideline-conform deficits for heavy users. A sensible use is a check for users with normal BMI or high training load, not a global floor.
- 30 kcal/kg FFM is of the same order as resting metabolic rate for many people, which is probably the root of the folk rule "never eat below your BMR". As a design rule "target intake not below estimated BMR" is a workable, body-size-scaled alternative to fixed floors for normal-weight users, but it rests on this inference only.
- Suggested numbers: keep absolute floors of 1200/1500 kcal as the last line; add a relative guard (no target more than about 25 to 30 % below expenditure, section 2) which in practice triggers first for most users; refuse a loss goal or show a clear state when BMI is below 18.5, and treat BMI 18.5 to 20 as a zone where a loss goal buys no health benefit (mortality data above).

### Gaps

- The origin of the 1200 kcal number could not be traced to a primary source. The ACSM position stand of 1983 ("Proper and improper weight loss programs", Med Sci Sports Exerc 15:ix-xiii, PMID 6341748), often named as the source of "not below 1200 kcal", has no abstract in Europe PMC and was not read.
- AHA/ACC/TOS 2013 and NHLBI 1998 calorie ranges were verified through secondary sources only; secondary sources disagree on the NHLBI men's range (1,200 to 1,500 vs 1,200 to 1,600 kcal).
- No study was found that tests "intake below BMR" as a threshold for harm.
- No energy availability threshold for men is established; 25 kcal/kg FFM comes from one narrative review. The IOC 2023 full text was not read, so its exact position on thresholds is paraphrased from the abstract.
- No trial defines a safe minimum intake by body size for unsupervised dieting.
- WHO definition of underweight (BMI below 18.5) was not fetched from a primary source.

## 4. Metabolic adaptation, diet breaks, refeeds and reverse dieting: what should a tracker do?

### Takeaway

Expenditure falls during a diet mostly because the body gets smaller; the extra "adaptive" part is real but small in ordinary diets (typically some tens to about 100 kcal/day at rest, larger in total expenditure through less movement) and largely fades at a stable weight, with the Biggest Loser data as the extreme outlier. Diet breaks and refeeds do not reliably improve fat loss or lean mass (one positive trial in men with obesity, several null trials in trained people), and reverse dieting has no supporting evidence, so the useful response of a tracker is to re-estimate expenditure from logged intake and weight, not to prescribe rituals.

### Cited Findings

- Nunes et al. 2022 (Br J Nutr 127:451-469), systematic review, 33 studies, n = 2528: adaptive thermogenesis was reported in 27 studies, but was smaller or not significant in studies with stronger designs and when measured after a period of weight stabilisation: [Nunes 2022, PubMed 33762040](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33762040/)
- Martins et al. 2020 (Am J Clin Nutr 112:558-565), 171 women with overweight, mean loss 12 kg: adaptation at the level of resting metabolic rate was -54 ± 105 kcal/day right after the loss under weight-stable conditions, was no longer present at 1 and 2 years, and did not predict regain: [Martins 2020, PubMed 32386226](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32386226/)
- Müller et al. 2015 (Am J Clin Nutr 102:807-819), 32 non-obese men, 3 weeks at 50 % of energy needs: resting expenditure fell 266 kcal/day, of which 108 kcal/day was adaptive (72 kcal/day after correcting for organ masses); the adaptation appeared within the first 3 days and was related to insulin: [Müller 2015, PubMed 26399868](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26399868/)
- Leibel, Rosenbaum, Hirsch 1995 (N Engl J Med 332:621-628), inpatients, 18 obese and 23 never-obese: maintaining a weight 10 % or more below the initial weight lowered total energy expenditure by 6 ± 3 kcal per kg fat-free mass per day (never obese) and 8 ± 5 (obese) beyond what composition predicts; resting and non-resting expenditure each fell 3 to 4 kcal/kg FFM/day: [Leibel 1995, PubMed 7632212](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7632212/)
- Rosenbaum et al. 2008 (Am J Clin Nutr 88:906-912), 7 matched trios: the lower total and non-resting expenditure was similar in people who had kept off 10 % or more for over a year and in recent losers, so it persists: [Rosenbaum 2008, PubMed 18842775](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18842775/); in contrast with [Martins 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32386226/), who found no persisting adaptation of resting metabolic rate
- Redman et al. 2009 (CALERIE phase 1): total daily expenditure adjusted for body composition was 431 ± 51 kcal/day lower at month 3 and 240 ± 83 kcal/day lower at month 6 under 25 % restriction or a low-calorie diet, and physical activity fell; neither happened in the group that created half the deficit by exercise: [Redman 2009, PubMed 19198647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19198647/)
- Martin et al. 2007 (Obesity 15:2964-2973), same trial: adjusted resting metabolic rate fell at month 3 with 25 % restriction; free-living activity level fell in the diet-only groups: [Martin 2007, PubMed 18198305](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18198305/)
- Fothergill et al. 2016 (Obesity 24:1612-1619), 14 Biggest Loser participants: metabolic adaptation of -499 ± 207 kcal/day six years after the contest despite substantial regain: [Fothergill 2016, PubMed 27136388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136388/)
- Trexler, Smith-Ryan, Norton 2014 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 11:7), review for athletes: adaptation includes lower expenditure, hormonal changes and greater mitochondrial efficiency; they suggest the smallest deficit that still gives progress, and describe refeeds and reverse dieting as untested ideas: [Trexler 2014, PubMed 24571926](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24571926/)
- MATADOR (Byrne et al. 2018, Int J Obes 42:129-138), 51 men with obesity randomised, 16 weeks at 67 % of maintenance, continuous vs 2 weeks diet / 2 weeks energy balance (30 weeks): per protocol (19 vs 17) weight loss 9.1 ± 2.9 vs 14.1 ± 5.6 kg, fat loss 8.0 vs 12.3 kg, fat-free mass loss similar; composition-adjusted fall in resting expenditure -749 vs -360 kJ/day: [Byrne 2018, PubMed 28925405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/)
- ICECAP (Peos et al. 2021, Med Sci Sports Exerc 53:1685-1698), 61 resistance-trained adults, 12 weeks of restriction either continuous or with a 1-week break after every 3 weeks: no difference in fat mass, fat-free mass, resting expenditure or hormones; less hunger and desire to eat with breaks: [Peos 2021, PubMed 33587549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/)
- Campbell et al. 2020 (J Funct Morphol Kinesiol 5:19), 27 resistance-trained adults, 7 weeks at about 25 % deficit, 2 carbohydrate refeed days per week vs continuous: reported fat-free mass -0.4 vs -1.3 kg and RMR -38 vs -78 kcal: [Campbell 2020, PubMed 33467235](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467235/); a published reanalysis states that only dry fat-free mass differed between groups: [Peos, Brown, Vorland, Allison, Sainsbury 2020, PubMed 33467300](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467300/)
- Siedler et al. 2023 (J Hum Kinet 86:117-132), 38 resistance-trained women, 25 % deficit for 6 diet weeks, continuous or with a 1-week break after every 2 weeks: no differences in body composition or resting metabolic rate; disinhibition scores were better with breaks: [Siedler 2023, PubMed 37181269](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37181269/)
- Poon et al. 2024 (Nutr Rev 83:59-71), meta-analysis, 12 RCTs, n = 881, intermittent dieting with breaks vs continuous: no difference in body mass, fat mass or fat-free mass; resting metabolic rate fell less with breaks, mainly in people with overweight or obesity, not in resistance-trained people: [Poon 2024, PubMed 38193357](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38193357/)
- ISSN position stand 2017: "The collective body of intermittent caloric restriction research demonstrates no significant advantage over daily caloric restriction for improving body composition": [Aragon 2017, PubMed 28630601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/)
- Reverse dieting: Da Silva et al. 2025 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr 22), 49 trained adults, 5 % weight loss, then 15 weeks of stepwise reverse diet vs immediate maintenance vs ad libitum: no significant difference in weight or fat regain between strategies (authors call it preliminary): [Da Silva 2025, PMC12381988](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12381988/)

### Inferences

- Size for a calculation: plan for expenditure to drop by what the lower body mass predicts (recompute BMR from the current weight) plus an extra 0 to 100 kcal/day at rest; in total expenditure the extra gap can reach 200 to 400 kcal/day during active restriction (CALERIE), a large part of it from less movement. A static 7700 kcal forecast that ignores both will always promise more than happens.
- The app already estimates expenditure from intake and weight trend (`estimateExpenditure`). That is the evidence-conform answer to adaptation: it captures mass loss, adaptive thermogenesis, less movement and logging error in one number. Recomputing the suggested target from it every few weeks is better supported than any fixed "adaptation percentage".
- Diet breaks: defensible as an optional comfort feature (less hunger in ICECAP, better disinhibition in Siedler, better "efficiency" in MATADOR), not as a metabolic necessity. If offered, the studied form is 1 to 2 weeks at maintenance after 2 to 3 weeks of deficit, and the forecast must lengthen accordingly (MATADOR took 30 instead of 16 weeks).
- Refeed days and reverse dieting should not be recommended as effective: the one positive refeed trial did not hold up in reanalysis, and the only reverse-diet trial found nothing. After the goal is reached, moving straight to the newly estimated maintenance is as good as stepping up slowly.
- Contested: whether adaptation persists (Rosenbaum, Fothergill say yes; Martins, Nunes say it mostly vanishes at stable weight). The difference is partly definitional (resting vs total expenditure, measured in deficit vs at stable weight). Strong evidence that it does not explain most regain.
- Creating part of the deficit through activity avoided the measured adaptation in CALERIE: a reason to credit training and steps rather than only cutting intake (small trial, n = 48 over four groups).

### Gaps

- No trial tests an app that adjusts targets from observed weight and intake against a static target; the recommendation is an inference.
- The range of adaptive thermogenesis by calculation method (about -65 to -230 kcal/day in a Martins/Nunes group paper) was seen only in a search summary and is not cited.
- Johannsen 2012 (Biggest Loser at week 30, -504 kcal/day) was not re-verified in this session and is left out of the findings.
- Long-term data on diet breaks in women with obesity and on breaks beyond 30 weeks are missing.

## 5. Weight regain, adherence, self-monitoring and safeguards (minimum BMI, eating disorder risk)

### Takeaway

How fast the weight comes off hardly changes how much comes back (about 70 % regain within 3 years after both rapid and gradual loss in the best trial), and faster early loss predicts better, not worse, long-term results in people with obesity; what predicts success is consistent self-monitoring. Calorie trackers are linked to eating disorder symptoms in cross-sectional studies of vulnerable groups, causality is unproven, so an app should monitor without pushing: no loss goals below normal weight, floors, and no rewards for undereating.

### Cited Findings

- Purcell et al. 2014 (Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2:954-962), RCT, n = 200, BMI 30 to 45: 12-week rapid programme (very-low-energy diet, 450 to 800 kcal) vs 36-week gradual programme (500 kcal deficit), target 15 % loss. 81 % vs 50 % reached at least 12.5 % loss; after the 144-week maintenance phase both groups had regained about 71 % (completers 70.5 % vs 71.2 %): [Purcell 2014, PubMed 25459211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25459211/)
- Nackers, Ross, Perri 2010 (Int J Behav Med 17:161-167), 262 women with obesity (mean BMI 36.8), grouped by first-month rate: fast (at least 0.68 kg/week), moderate, slow (under 0.23 kg/week) lost 13.5, 8.9 and 5.1 kg at 6 months and 10.9, 7.1 and 3.7 kg at 18 months; regain did not differ; the fast group was 5.1 times more likely to hold a 10 % loss at 18 months: [Nackers 2010, PubMed 20443094](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20443094/)
- Astrup and Rössner 2000 (Obes Rev 1:17-19): greater initial weight loss is associated with better long-term maintenance; they argue against the belief that fast initial loss leads to more regain: [Astrup 2000, PubMed 12119640](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12119640/)
- Burke, Wang, Sevick 2011 (J Am Diet Assoc 111:92-102), systematic review of 22 studies: a consistent, significant association between self-monitoring (diet, activity, weight) and weight loss; the evidence level is weak (self-report, homogeneous samples, no adherence standard): [Burke 2011, PubMed 21185970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/)
- Harvey et al. 2019 (Obesity 27:380-384), n = 142 in an online programme: participants who lost at least 5 % logged more often per day (2.4 vs 1.6 times in month 6); the time spent fell from 23.2 min/day in month 1 to 14.6 min/day in month 6. Frequency, not time or detail, predicted success: [Harvey 2019, PubMed 30801989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/)
- Wing and Phelan 2005 (Am J Clin Nutr 82:222S-225S): about 20 % of people with overweight keep off at least 10 % for a year; National Weight Control Registry members lost 33 kg on average and kept it for over 5 years, with about 1 hour of activity a day, regular self-weighing, a consistent eating pattern and breakfast; maintenance gets easier after 2 to 5 years: [Wing 2005, PubMed 16002825](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002825/)
- Butryn et al. 2007 (Obesity 15:3091-3096), NWCR, n = 3003: members whose self-weighing frequency fell over a year regained 4.0 kg vs 1.1 to 1.8 kg in those who kept or increased it: [Butryn 2007, PubMed 18198319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18198319/)
- Hernández-Reyes et al. 2020 (Front Psychol 11:397), RCT, 54 men with overweight or obesity, 6 months: weight self-monitoring with feedback gave more weight and fat loss than the same advice without it: [Hernández-Reyes 2020, PubMed 32210897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32210897/)
- Paixão et al. 2020 (Obes Rev 21:e13003), review of weight control registries in several countries: successful maintainers share regular self-monitoring, high activity and dietary consistency: [Paixão 2020, PubMed 32048787](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32048787/)
- Hall and Kahan 2018 (Med Clin North Am 102:183-197): weight loss plateaus at about 6 months because appetite rises and adherence drifts, more than because of metabolic slowing; long-term maintenance needs continued support: [Hall 2018, PubMed 29156185](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29156185/)
- Greenway 2015 (Int J Obes 39:1188-1196), review: appetite hormones and energy homeostasis shift after weight loss in ways that favour regain: [Greenway 2015, PubMed 25896063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25896063/)
- German S3 guideline 2024: regular self-observation (weighing, recording intake) is recommended for weight maintenance; new recommendation 5.37 (grade 0): digital applications and wearables can be used for self-monitoring; a comorbid eating disorder should be diagnosed and treated (grade B): [AWMF S3 Adipositas 2024 (PDF)](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/050-001l_S3_Praevention-Therapie-Adipositas_2024-10.pdf)
- Levinson, Fewell, Brosof 2017 (Eat Behav 27:14-16), 105 patients with a diagnosed eating disorder: about 75 % had used MyFitnessPal, and 73 % of those users felt it had contributed to their disorder: [Levinson 2017, PubMed 28843591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)
- Simpson and Mazzeo 2017 (Eat Behav 26:89-92), 493 college students, cross-sectional: use of calorie trackers was associated with higher eating concern and dietary restraint, fitness trackers with more eating disorder symptoms: [Simpson 2017, PubMed 28214452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28214452/)
- Linardon and Messer 2019 (Eat Behav 33:13-17), 122 men, cross-sectional: 56 % used MyFitnessPal, nearly 40 % of users felt it contributed to disordered eating; users had markedly higher eating disorder scores than non-users: [Linardon 2019, PubMed 30772765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30772765/)
- Hahn et al. 2021 (Body Image 39:221-226), qualitative follow-up of an experimental study in college women: experiences with dietary self-monitoring varied widely, from helpful awareness to preoccupation and guilt: [Hahn 2021, PubMed 34534770](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34534770/)
- ICECAP (trained adults, 12 weeks of dieting with tracking) found no group differences in eating disorder behaviours and no signal of worsening: [Peos 2021, PubMed 33587549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/)
- Physique athletes show higher rates of disordered eating and body image concerns, which contest-style dieting may worsen: [Roberts 2020, PubMed 32148575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148575/)
- Mortality is higher below BMI 20 (HR 1.13 at 18.5 to under 20, 1.51 at 15 to under 18.5): [Global BMI Mortality Collaboration 2016, PubMed 27423262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27423262/)

### Inferences

- "Slow and steady prevents regain" is not supported for people with obesity (Purcell, Nackers, Astrup): strong evidence by RCT and cohort. The argument for slow loss is lean mass and performance in lean users (section 1), not regain. The app need not steer heavy users to the slowest option.
- Adherence signal for design: frequency and consistency of logging matter, precision and time do not (Harvey). Quick entry paths, copy-yesterday and suggestions serve exactly this; a weekly weigh-in nudge is supported by NWCR data.
- Regain is the norm (about 70 % in 3 years in a supervised trial). A maintenance mode after the goal (keep logging and weighing, new maintenance target from the expenditure estimate) is more evidence-conform than ending the plan.
- Eating disorder risk: the evidence is cross-sectional and from selected groups, so it shows association and plausible harm for vulnerable users, not that tracking causes disorders; the one trial context (ICECAP) saw no worsening. Marked as contested, low to moderate evidence.
- Safeguards that follow from the cited facts (design inferences, not tested interventions): no loss goal when BMI is under 18.5, and a goal weight never below BMI 18.5 (consider 20 as the lowest suggested goal); the intake floors and rate caps of sections 1 to 3; no praise, streaks or green colour for eating far below the target (the app already has no streaks); show persistent intake far below target as a neutral state, not an achievement; keep the option to use the diary without a calorie goal. Age under 18 needs separate rules (not researched here).

### Gaps

- An experimental study in undergraduate women reportedly found no causal effect of one month of MyFitnessPal use on eating disorder symptoms; the citation could not be verified in this session and is not listed as a finding.
- No study evaluates specific in-app safeguards (BMI gates, floors, warning screens) for preventing disordered eating.
- No source was found that defines a minimum BMI for a goal weight; 18.5 is the WHO underweight border (primary WHO source not fetched) and 20 an inference from mortality data.
- Whether app-based tracking improves long-term (over 2 years) maintenance is not shown by RCTs.

## 6. Time-to-goal predictions: should the app warn that loss slows down?

### Takeaway

Yes: the linear 7700 kcal/kg (3500 kcal/lb) rule systematically overpredicts loss because expenditure falls as weight falls; validated dynamic models say a permanent change of about 10 kcal/day moves steady-state weight by about 1 lb (about 22 kcal/day per kg), with half of the change reached in about a year and nearly all in about three. In practice most people plateau around month 6, so any goal date from a linear rule should be shown as an optimistic estimate and recomputed from real data.

### Cited Findings

- Hall et al. 2011 (Lancet 378:826-837): a mathematical model validated against feeding studies shows that "the bodyweight response to a change of energy intake is slow, with half times of about 1 year"; people with more body fat lose more for the same intake change and take longer to reach the new steady state: [Hall 2011, PubMed 21872751](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21872751/)
- Rule of thumb from that paper as reported in the press: every permanent change of 100 kJ/day leads to about 1 kg of weight change (about 10 kcal/day per pound), half of it in about 1 year and 95 % in about 3 years (secondary source only): [FoodNavigator-USA 2011](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2011/09/01/Study-questions-calories-in-calories-out-equation-for-body-weight)
- Thomas et al. 2013 (Int J Obes 37:1611-1613): against seven controlled weight-loss experiments the 3500 kcal per pound rule grossly overestimated the actual loss; dynamic models fitted well: [Thomas 2013, PubMed 23628852](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23628852/)
- Thomas et al. 2014 (J Acad Nutr Diet 114:857-861): explains why the 3500 kcal rule fails (it ignores the falling expenditure) and points to validated dynamic predictors as replacement: [Thomas 2014, PubMed 24699137](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24699137/)
- Hall 2008 (Int J Obes 32:573-576): 32.2 MJ/kg (7700 kcal/kg) is right only above about 30 kg of initial body fat; for leaner people a kg of loss contains less energy, and the value changes during the loss: [Hall 2008, PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- German S3 guideline 2024: about 0.5 kg/week from a 500 to 600 kcal deficit only for at most three months, new balance after 3 to 6 months, 5 to 6 kg expected at 12 months: [AWMF S3 Adipositas 2024 (PDF)](https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/050-001l_S3_Praevention-Therapie-Adipositas_2024-10.pdf)
- Finkler et al. 2012: realised rates in studies are below the prescribed 0.5 to 1 kg/week and fall with duration: [Finkler 2012, PubMed 22717178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22717178/)
- Hall and Kahan 2018: the typical plateau at 6 months is driven mainly by rising appetite and waning adherence; the model shows that people would keep losing for years if intake stayed where it was set: [Hall 2018, PubMed 29156185](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29156185/)
- Label and logging error adds noise: restaurant foods stated energy accurately on average, but 19 % of items had at least 100 kcal more per portion than stated, and low-calorie items were understated most: [Urban 2011, PubMed 21771989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21771989/)

### Inferences

- Size of the error (my arithmetic): a constant 500 kcal/day deficit promises 23.7 kg in a year by the 7700 rule. The guideline expects 5 to 6 kg in real programmes; Hall's rule gives a final change of about 22.7 kg for 500 kcal (at 22 kcal/day per kg), but only about half, roughly 11 kg, in the first year, and only if intake really stays fixed. A linear forecast is fair for the first 4 to 12 weeks and increasingly wrong after.
- 7700 kcal/kg remains a fair conversion for short horizons in users with plenty of fat. For lean users the first kilograms hold less energy (more water, glycogen and lean tissue), so early loss looks faster than fat loss really is; a tracker should not extrapolate the first two weeks.
- Usable approach without a full dynamic model: keep the intake target constant only between re-estimates; each time the weight has moved by a few kg, recompute expenditure (BMR from current weight or the app's own expenditure estimate) and with it either the target or the date. That reproduces the main term of the dynamic models (expenditure falls roughly 20 to 25 kcal/day per kg lost, from Hall's rule).
- Wording for the UI: show the date as a range or "frühestens", and derive it from the measured trend (`weightTrend`) once enough data exist, since the trend already contains adherence and adaptation.
- Consensus, strong evidence (validated models, controlled feeding data): linear rules overpredict. Open: how much of the real-world plateau is physiology vs adherence; Hall attributes most to adherence.

### Gaps

- The exact wording and numbers of Hall's rule of thumb were not read in the Lancet full text (publisher and PMC blocked); the abstract confirms only the half time of about one year.
- The free NIH Body Weight Planner and the Pennington predictor implement these models; their equations were not extracted here (Chow and Hall 2008, PLoS Comput Biol, was not re-read).
- No study was found on whether showing realistic (slower) forecasts improves adherence compared with optimistic linear ones.

## 7. Sex differences and age

### Takeaway

Men lose somewhat more weight than women on the same programme, mostly a matter of body size and composition, and reviews see no case for sex-specific strategies; the sex-specific lower limits are the female hormonal threshold (energy availability) and, in practice, smaller bodies. With age the priority shifts to protecting lean mass: diet-only weight loss in people over 50 costs a high share of muscle unless combined with resistance training.

### Cited Findings

- Williams et al. 2015 (Obes Rev 16:171-186), systematic review of 58 studies: men lost more weight than women in diet (effect size about 0.49) and diet-plus-exercise interventions (about 0.24); the authors see no evidence that men and women need different weight-loss strategies: [Williams 2015, PubMed 25494712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25494712/)
- Christensen et al. 2018 (Diabetes Obes Metab 20:2840-2851), PREVIEW, n = 2224 with overweight and prediabetes, 8 weeks on an 810 kcal low-energy diet: men lost 11.8 % and women 10.3 % of body weight; after adjustment for the weight lost, women lost more fat-free mass and bone mineral content, men improved more in metabolic markers: [Christensen 2018, PubMed 30088336](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30088336/)
- Hall 2008: body composition explains the sex difference; women carry more fat at the same weight, so a kg of their loss holds more energy and the same deficit gives less weight loss: [Hall 2008, PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- Leibel 1995: the fall in energy expenditure after weight loss was not related to sex or degree of adiposity: [Leibel 1995, PubMed 7632212](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7632212/)
- Women: LH pulsatility is disrupted below 30 kcal/kg lean body mass/day within 5 days: [Loucks 2003, PubMed 12519869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/). Men: short-term energy availability of 15 kcal/kg FFM lowered leptin and insulin but not testosterone: [Koehler 2016, PubMed 26852783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26852783/); trained women lost 30 % of serum testosterone at a 1100 kcal deficit in 4 weeks: [Mero 2010, PubMed 20205751](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20205751/)
- Male cyclists show low bone density at the spine, lower testosterone and IGF-1 with chronic low energy availability: [Schofield 2021, PubMed 33108971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33108971/)
- Age 50 and older: 81 % of diet-only groups vs 39 % of diet-plus-exercise groups lost at least 15 % of their weight loss as fat-free mass: [Weinheimer 2010, PubMed 20591106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20591106/)
- Older adults with obesity: resistance training prevented 93.5 % of the lean mass loss from caloric restriction: [Sardeli 2018, PubMed 29596307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29596307/)
- Verreijen et al. 2017 (Nutr J 16:10), RCT, 100 adults aged 55 to 80 with overweight or obesity, 10-week hypocaloric diet: neither higher protein (achieved 1.13 vs 0.98 g/kg) nor resistance exercise alone changed fat-free mass significantly; only the combination increased it (+0.6 ± 1.3 kg): [Verreijen 2017, PubMed 28166780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28166780/)
- Finkler 2012: the rate of weight loss in published studies was higher in older and heavier subjects: [Finkler 2012, PubMed 22717178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22717178/)
- Mortality by BMI is flatter in older people (hazard ratio per 5 BMI units above 25: 1.52 at 35 to 49 years vs 1.21 at 70 to 89 years): [Global BMI Mortality Collaboration 2016, PubMed 27423262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27423262/)

### Inferences

- A percent-of-body-weight rate and a percent-of-expenditure deficit already absorb most of the sex difference, because it is a size and composition effect. No separate rate tables for men and women are needed (consensus, Williams 2015).
- The sex-specific floors (1200/1500) work as a size proxy. If the app adopts a body-size-scaled guard (BMR or energy availability check), the fixed pair can remain as a backstop only.
- Women at normal weight who train are the group with the best documented harm threshold (30 kcal/kg FFM). For them the slower options (0.25 to 0.5 %/week) should be the default.
- Older users (from about 60 to 65): prefer the moderate deficit (not above 500 kcal/day or about 0.5 %/week), since the benefit of weight loss per BMI unit is smaller and the lean mass cost higher; the German guideline's higher protein reference above 65 (1.0 g/kg) points the same way. The app cannot prescribe training, but the data justify treating logged strength training as the condition under which faster options are reasonable.

### Gaps

- No trial compares rates of loss by sex in lean, trained people (Garthe mixed sexes, Mero women only).
- No age-specific rate recommendation exists in the guidelines read; the suggestion for older users is an inference.
- Menopause, pregnancy, breastfeeding and adolescents were out of scope and not researched; an app needs separate rules or an exclusion there.
- The German S3 guideline's exact protein wording for weight reduction (1.2 to 2 g/kg) was read only in part; left to the protein researcher.
