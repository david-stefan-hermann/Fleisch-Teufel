# Energy surplus for muscle gain: how large, how fast, for whom (state: November 2026)

Reading note: "Verified" means I read the primary source or its abstract in this session. "Secondary" means the number comes from a summary of the paper and was not checked against the full text. Conversions kJ to kcal use 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ and are mine unless stated. Arithmetic marked "my calculation" is not from the sources.

Short verdict on the owner's belief ("muscle gain needs no surplus or only a very small one"): **partly correct**. Correct for beginners, detrained people and people with higher body fat (they gain muscle at maintenance and even in a moderate deficit). Largely supported, but not proven, for trained people: the controlled trials since 2013 consistently show that a larger surplus adds mainly fat, and no trial has shown a clear hypertrophy benefit of a surplus over maintenance in trained lifters, but all of these trials are small and short, so a small benefit of a small surplus cannot be excluded. Not correct as a blanket statement "no surplus is ever useful": a deficit measurably impairs lean mass gain, energy balance cannot be hit exactly, and reviews and position stands (Slater 2019, Iraki 2019, Aragon & Schoenfeld 2020, ISSN 2017) still recommend a small surplus to make sure one is not accidentally in a deficit. The current app options (+275 and +550 kcal/day for everyone) sit at the upper end or above what the literature recommends, especially +0.5 kg/week for light, female or advanced users.

## 1. Is a surplus required? Slater et al. 2019 and the theoretical energy cost of muscle

### Takeaway

The required surplus is unknown; the stored energy in 1 kg of muscle is small (about 1 200 kcal), the estimated total deposition cost is about 1 450 to 1 800 kcal per kg, and Slater's 1 500 to 2 000 kJ/day (about 360 to 480 kcal) is an explicitly conservative, unvalidated starting value from textbooks, not a measured requirement.

### Cited Findings

- Slater GJ, Dieter BP, Marsh DJ, Helms ER, Shaw G, Iraki J (2019). "Is an Energy Surplus Required to Maximize Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy Associated With Resistance Training". Front Nutr 6:131, DOI 10.3389/fnut.2019.00131. Narrative review, open access. Verified. Source: [PMC6710320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Quote: "the specific energy surplus required to facilitate muscle hypertrophy is unknown." Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Composition assumed: 1 kg skeletal muscle is about 75 % water, 20 % protein, 5 % fat, glycogen, minerals. "the energy stored in 1 kg of muscle is ~5,000-5,200 kJ" (about 1 200 to 1 240 kcal): about 3 400 kJ protein, 1 400 to 1 500 kJ fat, 300 to 450 kJ glycogen. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Deposition cost estimates quoted there: Forbes et al. 1986: 7 440 kJ per kg skeletal muscle (about 1 780 kcal); Joosen & Westerterp: about 6 050 kJ/kg (about 1 450 kcal), based on 29.4 kJ per g protein deposited; protein synthesis itself costs about 3.6 kJ per g. "These estimates have never been validated in a resistance training population" and "there is yet to be defined a single evidence-based energy estimate for accretion of 1 kg of SMM." Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Recommendation: "practitioners are advised to take a conservative approach to creating an energy surplus, within the range of ~1,500-2,000 kJ·day−1" (about 360 to 480 kcal/day), with monitoring of body composition and adjustment. Textbooks cited there go up to an extra 4 000 kJ/day for hard gainers. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- On gaining without surplus: "skeletal muscle hypertrophy is possible in an energy deficit, but we propose this response may be more likely among resistance training naive, overweight, or obese individuals." Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- The surplus is partly lost before storage: diet-induced thermogenesis rises about 1.1 kJ/h per 100 kJ extra mixed diet (Quatela); NEAT rose 1 380 ± 1 080 kJ/day in people overfed 4 200 kJ/day for 56 days (Levine); across overfeeding studies 60 to 70 % of the excess energy was stored (Müller). A typical resistance session costs little (3 sets of 10 at 70 % 1RM on five upper-body exercises: 369 ± 174 kJ, Mookerjee). Maintaining 1 kg muscle costs about 54 kJ/day. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Source of the surplus: "there does not appear to be any metabolic or functional benefit to the source of the energy surplus", provided protein (about 1.6 g/kg/day), carbohydrate and fat minimums are met. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- A 2026 primary paper states the mechanistic argument more sharply: "building even a kilo of muscle per month is estimated to require less than 100 kcal per day. This energy could be obtained by catabolizing fat mass, making it questionable whether an energy surplus is needed in the first place" (citing Slater 2019). Source: [Henselmans et al. 2026, Nutrients 18(12):1961](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961)

### Inferences

- My calculation: 1 kg muscle per month at 1 450 to 1 780 kcal/kg deposition cost is 48 to 59 kcal/day; even a fast novice rate of 1 kg lean mass per month needs well under 100 kcal/day for the tissue itself. The 360 to 480 kcal/day recommendation is therefore several times the tissue cost; the difference is a safety margin against undereating, measurement error of maintenance, and adaptive expenditure (TEF, NEAT), not physiology of muscle building.
- Slater 2019 is often quoted as "you need 350 to 500 kcal"; the paper itself says the need is unknown and frames the number as a conservative start to be individualised. Two of its co-authors (Helms, Dieter) later ran the 2023 trial that found no hypertrophy benefit of 5 % or 15 % surplus over maintenance.

### Gaps

- No study has directly measured the energy cost of resistance-training-induced muscle accretion in humans. All figures are derived from tissue composition or from growth and overfeeding studies without training.

## 2. Trials comparing surplus sizes (Rozenek 2002, Garthe 2013, Ribeiro 2019, Smith 2021, Helms 2023, Sanchez 2024, Henselmans 2026; overfeeding without training: Bray 2012, Leaf & Antonio 2017)

### Takeaway

In trained people every controlled comparison since 2013 found that the larger surplus produced clearly more fat and no statistically reliable extra muscle; only in untrained men (Rozenek 2002) and one tiny pilot in bodybuilders (Ribeiro 2019) did more energy go with more fat-free mass. All trials are small (n = 11 to 39) and short (4 to 12 weeks), so "no detectable benefit" is not "proven no benefit".

### Cited Findings

- **Rozenek R, et al. 2002**, J Sports Med Phys Fitness 42(3):340-7. 73 healthy (untrained) men, 8 weeks, training 4 days/week; carbohydrate/protein supplement (2 010 kcal: 356 g carbohydrate, 106 g protein) vs isocaloric carbohydrate vs no supplement. Body mass +3.1 ± 3.1 kg and +3.1 ± 2.2 kg; fat-free mass +2.9 ± 3.4 kg and +3.4 ± 2.5 kg in the supplement groups, both more than control; no between-group difference in strength. Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 12094125](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12094125/)
- Control group in Rozenek (no diet change): +0.6 kg body mass, −0.8 kg fat, +1.4 kg fat-free mass, i.e. untrained men recomposed at habitual intake. Secondary (MacroFactor summary of the paper). Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Critique of Rozenek: both supplement groups ate more protein than control, "making it unclear whether the additional protein or energy intake stimulated the increase in fat-free mass", and the equal strength gains raise "questions about whether the greater fat-free mass was contractile tissue". Source: [Henselmans et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961)
- **Garthe I, Raastad T, Refsnes PE, Sundgot-Borgen J 2013**, Eur J Sport Sci 13(3):295-303, DOI 10.1080/17461391.2011.643923. RCT, 39 elite athletes, 8 to 12 weeks, nutritional counselling with planned positive energy balance (NCG) vs ad libitum (ALG). Energy intake 3 585 ± 601 vs 2 964 ± 884 kcal; body weight +3.9 % vs +1.5 %; fat mass +15 % vs +3 %; lean mass gain not different between groups; 1RM improved in both. Authors: excess energy "should be considered carefully due to undesirable increases in body fat". Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 23679146](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23679146/)
- Garthe 2013 in absolute numbers as reported by Iraki 2019: lean mass +1.7 kg (surplus group, about 600 kcal more) vs +1.2 kg (ad libitum), not significant; fat mass +1.1 kg vs +0.2 kg, significant; Iraki et al. conclude that 200 to 300 kcal/day may suit highly trained athletes better than 500 kcal. Source: [Iraki 2019, PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710)
- Garthe 2013 as rates (secondary): 0.12 kg/week (0.16 % body weight/week, about 85 % of the gain lean) vs 0.27 kg/week (0.38 %/week, about 65 % lean); protein 1.7 vs 2.4 g/kg. Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Garthe 2011 follow-up (Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 36(4):547-54): after 6 and 12 months the counselled group had kept greater gains in body mass and lean mass than the ad libitum group. Abstract-level only. Source: [PubMed 21851204](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21851204)
- **Ribeiro AS, et al. 2019**, J Hum Kinet, DOI 10.2478/hukin-2019-0038. Pilot, 11 male competitive bodybuilders, 4 weeks, higher energy (67.5 kcal/kg/day, n = 6) vs moderate energy (n = 5; the moderate value, about 50 kcal/kg/day from memory, was not verified here). Muscle mass +2.7 % vs +1.1 %; body fat +7.4 % vs +0.8 %. Muscle estimated from anthropometry and skinfolds, energy intake self-reported. Verified (abstract). Source: [PMC6942464](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6942464)
- **Smith RW, et al. 2021**, J Funct Morphol Kinesiol, "Predicting Adaptations to Resistance Training Plus Overfeeding Using Bayesian Regression: A Preliminary Investigation". 28 resistance-trained men (21 completers), 6 weeks supervised training plus high-calorie supplement, target gain ≥ 0.45 kg/week, no maintenance control. Fat-free mass (4-compartment) +4.8 ± 2.6 %, elbow flexor thickness +4.5 ± 5.9 %, knee extensor thickness +7.4 ± 8.4 %. Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 33919267](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33919267/), [PMC8167794](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8167794)
- Smith 2021 regression (secondary): below about 0.55 % body weight/week the average subject recomposed; faster gain meant more fat relative to fat-free mass; gain rate was only weakly related to muscle thickness change (negatively for elbow flexors). Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- **Helms ER, Spence AJ, Sousa C, Krieger J, Taylor S, Oranchuk DJ, Dieter BP, Watkins CM 2023**, Sports Med Open 9:102, DOI 10.1186/s40798-023-00651-y. Parallel groups, 21 trained lifters randomised (17 completers, COVID-19 losses), 8 weeks, training 3 days/week, maintenance vs 5 % surplus vs 15 % surplus. No evidence for a group effect on muscle thickness (biceps, triceps, quadriceps) or squat 1RM; bench 1RM rose more with 15 % than with 5 % (BF10 = 9.9) and maintenance (BF10 = 14.5); skinfolds rose more with 15 % than maintenance (BF10 = 4.2), weakly more with 5 % (BF10 = 2.4). Regression over all subjects: body mass change predicted skinfold change (BF10 = 14.3, R² = 0.49) and only weakly biceps thickness. Conclusion: "faster rates of BM gain (and by proxy larger surpluses) primarily increase rates of fat gain rather than augmenting 1-RM or MT." Caveat by the authors: mixed training experience, moderate volume, 8 weeks. Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 37914977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/), [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/ab6ce09af2cc4daa9a1c2e9b88e1b1e9)
- Helms 2023, secondary detail: targets corresponded to about 1 % and 3 % body weight per month; gain rate was unrelated to quadriceps and triceps growth (R² 0 to 0.08), weakly related to biceps growth (R² 0.24). Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- **Sanchez AD, et al. 2024**, Med Sci Sports Exerc 56(8):1454-1466, "A Randomized Trial of Healthy Weight Gain in Athletic Individuals". 19 male and 13 female athletes, +500 kcal/day above habitual intake (peanut snacks vs high-carbohydrate snacks), supervised whole-body training 3 days/week, 10 weeks, DXA. Overall +2.2 ± 1.3 kg body mass, of which 1.5 ± 1.1 kg lean (about 70 %); peanut group +1.6 ± 1.1 kg vs carbohydrate group +2.7 ± 1.2 kg (P = 0.007), lean mass +1.2 vs +1.9 kg (P = 0.136). About 0.22 kg/week "primarily as LBM" in both sexes; individual range −0.9 to +4.4 kg. No maintenance control. Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 38537251](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38537251/), [ACSM author summary](https://www.acsm.org/healthy-weight-gain)
- **Henselmans M, Tiede DR, Plotkin DL, et al. 2026**, Nutrients 18(12):1961, DOI 10.3390/nu18121961. Quasi-randomised, double-blind, counterbalanced crossover, 20 resistance-trained men (26.7 ± 4.9 years, 9.7 ± 6.1 years training), 8 weeks per condition; protein-only supplement vs protein plus 50 g carbohydrate. The carbohydrate condition had +485 kcal/day (p = 0.017, self-reported). No significant difference in DXA lean mass, ultrasound muscle thickness and cross-sectional area, squat 1RM, fatigue index or peak torque. Authors: "higher energy surpluses may not enhance muscle hypertrophy compared to energy-maintenance conditions", but "Our findings should not be interpreted as true null effects. The wide confidence intervals do not rule out a (small) positive (or negative) effect"; habitual diet, self-report and possible compensation are limitations; lean and fat mass rose non-significantly more with the extra energy. Verified (full text PDF). Source: [Nutrients 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961)
- The same paper summarises the field: "multiple studies have not observed significantly greater resistance training-induced neuromuscular adaptations resulting from increased energy intakes outside of energy deficit, even when the higher energy intakes resulted in significantly greater fat gain" (refs: Sanchez 2024, Hambre 2012, Helms 2023, Garthe 2013, Garthe 2011). Source: [Nutrients 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961)
- **Bray GA, et al. 2012**, JAMA 307(1):47-55. Inpatient RCT, 25 adults (number from memory, not verified here), overfeeding 954 kcal/day (95 % CI 884 to 1 022; about 40 % above maintenance) for 8 weeks with 5 %, 15 % or 25 % protein, **no resistance training**. Weight gain 3.16 kg (low protein) vs 6.05 kg (normal) vs 6.51 kg (high), P = 0.002; body fat rose similarly in all groups and accounted for 50 % to over 90 % of the stored excess energy; resting energy expenditure rose by 160 and 227 kcal/day with normal and high protein. Verified (abstract). Source: [PubMed 22215165](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)
- Bray 2012 as read by Slater: fat gain about 3.5 kg in all groups, lean body mass gain about 3 kg only with the two higher protein intakes. Twin overfeeding study (Bouchard 1990, ref. 45 in Slater): 353 MJ surplus over 100 days, +5.4 kg fat and +2.7 kg fat-free mass, about 2 kg fat per 1 kg lean, without training. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- **Leaf A, Antonio J 2017**, Int J Exerc Sci 10(8):1275-1296, narrative review of overfeeding. Only 25 overfeeding studies reported fat mass and fat-free mass separately; protein overfeeding differs from carbohydrate or fat overfeeding: a high-protein diet "may not" increase body weight or fat mass even above habitual intake. Abstract-level only. Source: [PubMed 29399253](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29399253/), [PMC5786199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5786199)

### Inferences

- Pattern across trials: lean share of the gain falls as the rate rises (Garthe: about 85 % lean at 0.16 %/week vs about 65 % at 0.38 %/week; Sanchez: about 70 to 75 % lean at 0.2 to 0.4 %/week; Smith: recomposition on average below about 0.55 %/week in a 6-week study of men with at least 6 months of training). A defensible planning value is 65 to 75 % lean at 0.25 to 0.4 % body weight per week over 8 to 12 weeks, lower for advanced lifters and longer periods.
- The statement "more surplus gives no more muscle" rests on trials with 17 to 39 participants. Point estimates often lean slightly towards the larger surplus (Garthe +0.5 kg lean, Sanchez +0.7 kg lean, Helms biceps, Henselmans non-significant lean mass), so the honest reading is: any hypertrophy benefit beyond a small surplus is small and bought with disproportionate fat gain.
- Short-term fat-free mass gains from overfeeding include water and glycogen; equal strength gains in Rozenek, Garthe and Henselmans suggest the extra fat-free mass is not all contractile tissue.
- Overfeeding without training (Bray, twin study) stores about two thirds of the gain as fat, and even there adequate protein decides whether lean mass rises: protein adequacy matters more than surplus size.

### Gaps

- No meta-analysis of surplus size vs hypertrophy was found for 2023 to 2026; the evidence base is about five to seven small trials. MacroFactor counts "five" controlled bulking studies as of October 2024.
- Helms 2023 group-level body mass and muscle thickness changes, sex split and actual surplus adherence could not be read (Springer and Research Square pages blocked); only the abstract values are verified.
- No trial longer than 12 weeks, none in trained women only, none stratified by body fat.
- Bray 2012 lean mass values per group (commonly quoted: −0.70, +2.87, +3.18 kg) were not verified against the paper.
- Stronger by Science / MASS write-ups of these trials were not opened individually; MacroFactor (same authors) was used as the pointer.

## 3. Muscle gain at maintenance or in a deficit (recomposition)

### Takeaway

A deficit impairs lean mass gain in a dose-dependent way (about 500 kcal/day abolishes it on average in mostly older, untrained, overweight samples), but gains at maintenance and in small deficits are well documented; recomposition is likeliest for untrained, detrained or higher-body-fat people with high protein, and is documented but harder in trained, lean people.

### Cited Findings

- **Murphy C, Koehler K 2022** (online 2021), Scand J Med Sci Sports 32(1):125-137, DOI 10.1111/sms.14075, "Energy deficiency impairs resistance training gains in lean mass but not strength: A meta-analysis and meta-regression". Authors' conclusion: "Individuals performing RT to build LM should avoid prolonged energy deficiency, and individuals performing RT to preserve LM during weight loss should avoid energy deficits >500 kcal day-1." Source: [Zendy record of the abstract](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.14075)
- Details (secondary, Stronger by Science review of the paper): Analysis A, head-to-head RCTs: 7 studies, 282 participants, mean age 60 ± 11, six studies women only: lean mass ES −0.57 (p = 0.02), strength ES −0.31 (p = 0.28). Analysis B, matched groups: 31 deficit studies matched within 52 studies, 1 213 participants, mean age 51 ± 16: lean mass ES −0.11 with deficit (p = 0.03) vs +0.20 without (p < 0.001); strength ES 0.84 vs 0.81. Meta-regression slope −0.00031 per kcal (p = 0.02), i.e. −0.031 ES per 100 kcal/day; predicted ES = 0 at about 500 kcal/day. Deficit groups had BMI 32.7 ± 3.0 vs 27.5 ± 3.6. Source: [Stronger by Science, "muscle caloric deficit"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- Limits stated by the authors themselves: almost all studies enrolled sedentary people; "on average, an energy deficit of 500 kcal per day resulted in neither a loss nor a gain in lean mass"; for experienced lifters the evidence is inconclusive. Source: [Koehler & Murphy on mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-i-gain-muscle-and-lose-weight-at-the-same-time)
- Critique: 500 kcal/day is not a hard cutoff; most studies with substantial lean gains had estimated deficits of about 200 to 300 kcal/day or less; protein intake was not controlled; the number should not be taken literally for lean or trained people. Source: [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- **Longland TM, Oikawa SY, Mitchell CJ, Devries MC, Phillips SM 2016**, Am J Clin Nutr 103(3):738-46. RCT, 40 young overweight recreationally active men, 4 weeks, about 40 % energy deficit (33 kcal/kg lean mass), resistance plus high-intensity interval training 6 days/week; protein 2.4 vs 1.2 g/kg/day. Lean mass +1.2 kg vs +0.1 kg; fat mass −4.8 ± 1.6 kg vs −3.5 ± 1.4 kg (P < 0.05). Verified (abstract; lean mass values via author interview). Source: [PubMed 26817506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/), [interview](https://medicalresearch.com/?p=21100)
- **Barakat C, Pearson J, Escalante G, Campbell B, De Souza EO 2020**, Strength Cond J 42(5):7-21, DOI 10.1519/SSC.0000000000000584, narrative review. Abstract: "Although many suggest that this only occurs in untrained/novice and overweight/obese populations, there is a substantial amount of literature demonstrating this body recomposition phenomenon in resistance-trained individuals." Source: [abstract record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4066252?lng=en)
- Barakat's tables document several studies where group averages show simultaneous fat loss and fat-free mass gain in trained participants. Secondary. Source: [MacroFactor on recomposition](https://macrofactor.com/recomposition/)
- Untrained men at habitual intake recomposed in Rozenek's control group (+1.4 kg fat-free mass, −0.8 kg fat in 8 weeks). Secondary. Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Who can recomp (practitioner ranking): most feasible for untrained people with substantial body fat (BMI over 30 an obvious candidate); still possible for trained people with BMI below 30 but needs tight training, nutrition and sleep; least feasible for lean people near their muscular ceiling. Suggested rates: recomposition with muscle priority +0.05 to +0.1 % body weight/week, with fat-loss priority −0.1 to −0.5 %/week. Source: [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- Slater 2019 agrees on the population: hypertrophy in a deficit "may be more likely among resistance training naive, overweight, or obese individuals". Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- ISSN position stand: higher protein (2.3 to 3.1 g/kg fat-free mass) may be needed to maximise muscle retention in lean, resistance-trained people in a deficit. Source: [ISSN position stand, Aragon et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)

### Inferences

- The dose-response from Murphy & Koehler is the strongest argument against "no surplus needed" taken literally: if a deficit linearly reduces lean gain, then energy status matters, and since day-to-day balance cannot be hit exactly, aiming at exactly zero means being in a deficit about half the time. A small surplus (order of 100 to 300 kcal/day) is insurance against that.
- The meta-regression does not show that going above zero keeps adding muscle; it is silent on surpluses.
- For app logic: "Recomposition (maintenance, high protein, training)" is a legitimate third option next to "gain" and "lose", best suited for beginners, returners and users with higher body fat.

### Gaps

- Barakat 2020 full text is paywalled; the often-quoted conditions (protein 1.6 to 2.4 g/kg and so on) appear only on secondary sites and are not verified.
- Murphy & Koehler numbers per analysis were taken from a secondary review, not the paper (publisher PDF blocked).
- No good data on how long trained people can keep recomposing; the trials last 4 to 12 weeks.

## 4. Realistic rates of muscle and weight gain by training status and sex

### Takeaway

Measured lean mass gain in studies averages about 1.5 kg in about 10 weeks for men (roughly 0.5 to 0.7 kg/month, novices more, trained less), and the published recommendations converge on 0.25 to 0.5 % of body weight per week for novices and intermediates and about 0.25 % or less for advanced lifters, with newer practitioner guidance for experienced lifters as low as 0.1 to 0.15 %/week.

### Cited Findings

- **Benito PJ, Cupeiro R, Ramos-Campo DJ, Alcaraz PE, Rubio-Arias JÁ 2020**, Int J Environ Res Public Health 17(4):1285, DOI 10.3390/ijerph17041285. Meta-analysis, 111 studies (158 groups), 1 927 healthy men aged 18 to 40. Pooled gain 1.53 kg muscle mass (95 % CI 1.30 to 1.76); fat-free mass +1.56 kg (1.23 to 1.89), lean mass +1.65 kg (1.28 to 2.01), skeletal muscle mass +1.11 kg (0.52 to 1.71); mean duration about 10 weeks. Untrained vs trained subgroups: fat-free mass +1.54 vs +0.98 kg, lean mass +1.70 vs +1.09 kg (training status not a significant moderator, p = 0.19). Mostly pre-post designs, mixed measurement methods. Verified. Source: [PMC7068252](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7068252/)
- Trial-level rates under a surplus: Sanchez 2024, +1.5 kg lean in 10 weeks in male and female athletes (0.15 kg lean/week); Garthe 2013, +1.2 to +1.7 kg lean in 8 to 12 weeks in elite athletes; Rozenek 2002, +2.9 to +3.4 kg fat-free mass in 8 weeks in untrained men with a very large surplus; Smith 2021, +4.8 % fat-free mass in 6 weeks in trained men gaining ≥ 0.45 kg/week. Sources: [Sanchez](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38537251/), [Garthe via Iraki](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710), [Rozenek](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12094125/), [Smith](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33919267/)
- **Iraki J, Fitschen P, Espinar S, Helms E 2019**, Sports 7(7):154, narrative review. "A hyper-energetic diet (~10-20%) should be consumed with a target weight gain of ~0.25-0.5% of bodyweight/week for novice/intermediate bodybuilders." Advanced: lower end or more conservative; Table 1 gives advanced about 0.25 % body weight/week and +5 to 10 % energy (the text says "lower end" of 10 to 20 %, a small inconsistency inside the paper). Protein 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg, fat 0.5 to 1.5 g/kg, carbohydrate the rest (at least 3 to 5 g/kg). A 2 kg monthly gain may be "too excessive" for advanced lifters. The authors say the off-season literature "is lacking". Verified. Source: [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710), [PubMed 31247944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/)
- **Helms, Morgan, Valdez, Muscle and Strength Pyramid: Nutrition** (book, expert opinion): recommended body weight gain per month: novice 1 to 1.5 %, intermediate 0.5 to 1 %, advanced up to 0.5 %; these are weight, part of it fat. Andy Morgan's updated article uses 2 % (beginner), 1.5 % (novice), 1 % (intermediate), 0.5 % (advanced) per month. Secondary (co-author's site, no primary data). Source: [RippedBody](https://rippedbody.com/?p=58796)
- **Alan Aragon's model** (expert opinion, muscle gain per month as % of body weight): beginner 1 to 1.5 %, intermediate 0.5 to 1 %, advanced 0.25 to 0.5 %. **Lyle McDonald's model**: about 2 lb (0.9 kg) of muscle per month in the first year of proper training, declining each year. Both are coaching heuristics for men without primary data; one summary calls Aragon's figures "a little over inflated". Secondary. Source: [Breaking Muscle](https://breakingmuscle.com/how-fast-should-you-gain-mass/), [RNT Fitness](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/)
- **MacroFactor / Stronger by Science (Nuckols, Trexler), revised October 2024** after the evidence base grew "from two studies to five". Weight gain in % of body weight per week (conservative / happy medium / aggressive / very aggressive): beginner 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8 / 1.0; intermediate 0.15 / 0.325 / 0.575 / 0.8; experienced 0.1 / 0.15 / 0.35 / 0.6. Absolute caps in kg/week: beginner 0.16 / 0.4 / 0.64 / 0.8; intermediate 0.12 / 0.26 / 0.46 / 0.64; experienced 0.08 / 0.12 / 0.28 / 0.48 (percent scaling breaks down for heavy people). Definitions: beginner = strength up at least 2 %/week (rarely beyond 3 to 6 months of serious training); intermediate = about 1 %/week (rarely beyond a year); experienced = well below 1 %/week (most people after 1 to 2 years). Expected composition: conservative = little or no fat gain, possibly recomposition; happy medium = two thirds to three quarters fat-free; very aggressive = at least 50 % may be fat. Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/), [October 2024 update](https://macrofactor.com/mm-october-2024/)
- Stronger by Science (Trexler) for hypertrophy: about 0.1 %/week for experienced and about 0.25 %/week for inexperienced lifters. Source: [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- **Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ 2020**, Strength Cond J 42(5):79-86, "Magnitude and Composition of the Energy Surplus for Maximizing Muscle Hypertrophy: Implications for Bodybuilding and Physique Athletes". Abstract-level: maximising hypertrophy requires an energy surplus; size depends on training status. Secondary summary of its numbers: novices may benefit from larger surpluses (above 500 kcal/day, about 20 to 25 % above expenditure), advanced lifters from 200 to 500 kcal/day (about 10 to 15 %). Full text paywalled (HTTP 402), numbers NOT verified. Source: [journal page](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2020/10000/magnitude_and_composition_of_the_energy_surplus.9.aspx), [secondary summary, Fit Generation](https://fitgeneration.es/es-necesario-hacer-volumen-para-ganar-musculo/)
- **ISSN position stand, Aragon AA et al. 2017**, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:16: "Diets focused primarily on accruing LM are driven by a sustained caloric surplus to facilitate anabolic processes and support increasing resistance-training demands. The composition and magnitude of the surplus, as well as training status of the subjects can influence the nature of the gains." No specific kcal figure verified. It notes "a paucity of research on women and older populations". Source: [DOI 10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)
- Sex: in Sanchez 2024 the +500 kcal protocol produced a similar gain (about 0.22 kg/week, mostly lean) "in both male and female athletes". Source: [PubMed 38537251](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38537251/)

### Inferences

- My calculation from Benito: 1.56 kg fat-free mass over 10.4 weeks is about 0.15 kg/week or 0.65 kg/month, as an average over mostly untrained to moderately trained young men early in a programme (includes water and glycogen, so it overstates contractile tissue). Trained subgroup: about 1.0 kg in about 10 weeks, roughly 0.4 kg/month.
- If realistic lean gain is 0.4 to 0.7 kg/month for most men (less for women in absolute terms because of lower body mass, and far less for advanced lifters), then a weight gain of 2 kg/month (the app's +0.5 kg/week) can be at most about one third lean for a typical trained user. The app's +0.25 kg/week (about 1 kg/month) matches novice or intermediate rates for a 70 to 100 kg person (0.25 to 0.36 %/week) and is too fast for advanced or light users.
- Rates should scale with body weight (percent per week) and training status, with an absolute cap, rather than fixed kg/week. For a 60 kg woman, +0.5 kg/week is 0.83 %/week, in MacroFactor's "aggressive to very aggressive" beginner band and beyond every published recommendation for intermediates.
- Convergence 2019 to 2026: older models (Aragon, Helms pyramid, Iraki) and newer data-driven ones (MacroFactor 2024) agree for novices (about 0.25 to 0.5 %/week); for experienced lifters the newer guidance is lower (0.1 to 0.15 %/week default) than Iraki's 0.25 %.

### Gaps

- Lyle McDonald's and Alan Aragon's original tables were not opened at the source; numbers come from secondary pages. McDonald's widely quoted yearly table (about 9 to 11 kg in year 1, half each following year, women about half) is from memory and not verified here.
- No verified empirical data on sex differences in the rate of gain was collected in this session. The commonly cited finding that relative (percent) hypertrophy is similar in women and men while absolute gains are smaller (Roberts et al. 2020 meta-analysis) was not verified here.
- Aragon & Schoenfeld 2020 numbers need the full text.

## 5. Partitioning: lean vs fat share of the gain (surplus size, training, protein, starting body fat, p-ratio)

### Takeaway

With training and adequate protein, about 65 to 85 % of a slow gain is lean in 8 to 12 week trials, and this share falls as the rate of gain rises; without training about two thirds is fat. The popular claim that leaner people partition a surplus better (bulk only at 10 to 15 % body fat) is not supported by direct evidence in resistance-trained people and is contested.

### Cited Findings

- Lean share vs rate in trained athletes: about 85 % lean at 0.16 % body weight/week vs about 65 % at 0.38 %/week (Garthe 2013); about 75 % vs 70 % lean at 0.16 vs 0.27 kg/week (Sanchez 2024); summary: 0.24 to 0.38 %/week gives about 70 to 75 % lean, 25 to 30 % fat. Secondary. Source: [MacroFactor bulking article](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Helms 2023: body mass gain explained 49 % of the variance in skinfold change and almost none of the muscle thickness change (R² 0 to 0.08 quadriceps and triceps, 0.24 biceps). Source: [PubMed 37914977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/), [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Untrained with a very large surplus: Rozenek 2002, nearly 100 % of a 3.1 kg gain was fat-free mass in 8 weeks (skinfold-based method, untrained men; water and glycogen included). Source: [PubMed 12094125](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12094125/), [Iraki 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710)
- Without training: twin overfeeding study +5.4 kg fat and +2.7 kg fat-free mass (about 67 % fat); Bray 2012: fat gain about 3.5 kg regardless of protein, lean gain about 3 kg only with 15 % or 25 % protein. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320), [Bray 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)
- Protein overfeeding behaves differently from carbohydrate or fat overfeeding (may not increase fat mass). Source: [Leaf & Antonio 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29399253/)
- Forbes' theory (the lean share of weight change depends on body fat) is implemented in Hall 2008: a larger cumulative deficit per kg is needed for people with more initial body fat, because lean people lose (and by the same curve gain) a larger lean fraction. Source: [Hall 2008, Int J Obes, PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/)
- Critique of the "p-ratio" bulking rule (Stronger by Science, Nuckols/Trexler): Forbes' data come from overfeeding and weight change without resistance training; in subject-level data from training studies, people with lower and higher starting body fat gained fat-free mass to a similar degree; one study (Schoenfeld et al., PMC5214805) even found a positive correlation between baseline body fat and lean gains; every subject under 8 % body fat gained fat, and only one gained more than 1 kg fat-free mass. Conclusion: the hypothesis that high body fat impairs muscle gain "is hard to justify with current evidence", though not disproven; the proposed 10 to 15 % "ideal" bulking range has no direct evidence. Menno Henselmans published the opposing view (insulin resistance and inflammation as the proposed mechanism). Source: [Stronger by Science, p-ratio rebuttal](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/p-ratios-rebuttal-2/)
- ISSN: "The composition and magnitude of the surplus, as well as training status of the subjects can influence the nature of the gains." Source: [ISSN 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)

### Inferences

- Well supported: lean share falls with faster gain; training and adequate protein (about 1.6 g/kg or more) are preconditions for a high lean share; novices have a higher lean share than advanced lifters at the same rate.
- Contested: any effect of starting body fat on partitioning during training. For an app, body fat should decide the goal for health and preference reasons (a user with high body fat is better served by recomposition or a cut), not because of a claimed partitioning penalty.
- Planning values (my synthesis): lean share about 80 % or more at up to 0.15 %/week, about 70 % at 0.25 to 0.4 %/week, about 50 to 65 % at 0.5 %/week and above for trained people; novices can sit one step higher. These are short-term trial values and include water and glycogen.

### Gaps

- No trial has randomised people by starting body fat to a surplus with training.
- The original Forbes papers (1986, 1987, 2000) were not opened; they are represented through Slater 2019, Hall 2008 and the Stronger by Science critique.
- Lean shares from Garthe and Sanchez per group are from a secondary summary.

## 6. Energy per kg gained: converting a rate (kg/week) into kcal/day

### Takeaway

7 700 kcal/kg is the energy density of adipose tissue; stored energy is about 9 400 kcal per kg of pure fat and about 1 800 kcal per kg of lean tissue, so a lean-dominated gain stores only about 3 300 to 5 600 kcal/kg. In practice more intake is needed than the stored energy because expenditure rises with overfeeding, so 7 700 kcal/kg overstates the stored energy of a good bulk but is not clearly too high as an intake prescription; the cleaner approach is to set the rate from body weight and training status and correct the surplus from the measured weight trend.

### Cited Findings

- Hall KD 2008, Int J Obes 32(3):573-6, "What is the required energy deficit per unit weight loss?": energy densities ρL = 7.6 MJ/kg lean tissue and ρF = 39.5 MJ/kg fat (about 1 815 and 9 440 kcal/kg). The 3 500 kcal/lb (7 700 kcal/kg) rule approximately fits only people with more than 30 kg initial body fat and overestimates the energy per kg for leaner people. Source: [PubMed 17848938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17848938/), [Nature page](https://www.nature.com/articles/0803720)
- Skeletal muscle specifically: about 5 000 to 5 200 kJ/kg stored (1 200 to 1 240 kcal); deposition cost estimates 6 050 to 7 440 kJ/kg (1 450 to 1 780 kcal). Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320)
- Losses before storage: 60 to 70 % of overfed energy is stored; NEAT rose on average 1 380 kJ/day (330 kcal) with very large individual spread (± 1 080 kJ) at 4 200 kJ/day overfeeding; resting expenditure rose 160 to 227 kcal/day in Bray's normal and high protein arms. Source: [Slater 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320), [Bray 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)
- Practitioner conversions: RippedBody (Morgan): muscle about 2 500 kcal/lb (5 500 kcal/kg), fat 3 500 kcal/lb; at a 1:1 ratio about 100 kcal/day per lb per month, plus 50 % for NEAT = about 150 kcal/day per lb/month (about 330 kcal/day per kg/month); the Muscle and Strength Pyramid book uses 3 500 kcal/lb for both. MacroFactor says its bulking surpluses are smaller than people expect because the 3 500 kcal rule (Wishnofsky 1958) does not describe lean-dominated gain, and it adjusts intake from the measured trend. Source: [RippedBody](https://rippedbody.com/?p=58796), [MacroFactor knowledge base (search result excerpt; page itself returned 404)](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/199-why-does-macrofactor-recommend-smaller-surpluses-for-bulking-than-i-expected)
- Empirical prescriptions vs outcome: +500 kcal/day prescribed for 10 weeks gave +2.2 kg (Sanchez 2024); +954 kcal/day for 8 weeks inpatient without training gave +6.05 kg with normal protein (Bray 2012); +2 010 kcal/day supplement for 8 weeks gave +3.1 kg (Rozenek 2002, free-living, habitual diet reportedly unchanged). Sources: [Sanchez](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38537251/), [Bray](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/), [Rozenek](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12094125/)

### Inferences (all my calculations)

- Stored energy per kg of gain with Hall's densities: 100 % lean 1 815; 80/20 lean/fat about 3 340; 70/30 about 4 100; 60/40 about 4 870; 50/50 about 5 630; 100 % fat 9 440 kcal/kg. 7 700 kcal/kg corresponds to a gain that is about 77 % fat and 23 % lean by this model (adipose tissue itself is about 87 % fat).
- Prescribed surplus per kg actually gained in the trials: Bray about 8 800 kcal/kg (53 400 kcal / 6.05 kg, metabolic ward, about half lean); Sanchez about 15 900 kcal/kg (35 000 kcal / 2.2 kg, free-living, snacks provided, compensation likely); Rozenek about 36 000 kcal/kg (clearly incomplete adherence or strong compensation). So real-world intake needed per kg gained is higher than the stored energy, typically by a factor of 1.5 to 2 or more, and very individual.
- Consequence for the app: (a) the theoretical surplus for a lean gain is small: 0.25 % of 80 kg per week = 0.2 kg/week at 4 100 kcal/kg stored is about 117 kcal/day; with a 50 % allowance for adaptive expenditure about 175 kcal/day; with 7 700 kcal/kg it is 220 kcal/day. All three are within the measurement error of maintenance estimates (±10 % of TDEE is 200 to 300 kcal). (b) The choice of the per-kg constant therefore matters less than choosing the right rate and correcting from the weight trend. (c) A defensible constant for "gain" is about 5 500 kcal/kg (RippedBody's muscle figure, and close to a 50/50 gain) to 7 700 kcal/kg; keeping 7 700 is conservative in the sense that the user reaches the target rate rather than falling short, at the price of slightly more fat if expenditure does not adapt.
- The app's current values: +0.25 kg/week = +275 kcal/day lies in the range Iraki derives from Garthe (200 to 300 kcal/day for trained athletes) and below Slater's 360 to 480 kcal/day. +0.5 kg/week = +550 kcal/day is above Slater's range and corresponds to the surplus sizes (500 to 600 kcal) that produced the extra fat without significant extra lean mass in Garthe 2013 and no measurable benefit in Henselmans 2026.

### Gaps

- No validated kcal-per-kg constant exists for weight gain with resistance training; no dynamic model (like Hall's for weight loss) has been validated for bulking with training.
- MacroFactor's exact internal constant was not found.

## 7. Practical consensus 2026: surplus by training status, lean bulk vs "maingaining", role of sex and body fat

### Takeaway

The 2026 consensus is a small surplus scaled to training status (roughly 5 to 20 % or 100 to 500 kcal/day, weight gain 0.1 to 0.5 % of body weight per week), with maintenance ("maingaining"/recomposition) a legitimate option for beginners, returners and people with higher body fat, and with a clear trend since 2023 towards smaller surpluses for trained lifters; whether a surplus beats true maintenance for trained lifters is unresolved.

### Cited Findings

- Reviews recommending a surplus: Slater 2019 (about 360 to 480 kcal/day, conservative start, need unknown); Iraki 2019 (+10 to 20 % novice/intermediate, +5 to 10 % or lower end for advanced; 0.25 to 0.5 % body weight/week, advanced about 0.25 %); ISSN 2017 (sustained surplus, size depends on training status); Aragon & Schoenfeld 2020 (surplus required to maximise hypertrophy; secondary: larger for novices, 200 to 500 kcal for advanced, unverified). Sources: [Slater](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320), [Iraki](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710), [ISSN](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y), [Aragon & Schoenfeld](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2020/10000/magnitude_and_composition_of_the_energy_surplus.9.aspx)
- Trial evidence since then points to "smaller is enough": Helms 2023 (5 % and 15 % no better than maintenance for muscle thickness over 8 weeks, more fat), Henselmans 2026 (+485 kcal/day, no significant difference in trained men, conclusion "higher energy surpluses may not enhance muscle hypertrophy compared to energy-maintenance conditions" with explicit caveat that a small effect is not ruled out). Sources: [Helms 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/), [Henselmans 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961)
- The open question stated by the Helms group: whether a larger surplus is more effective than a smaller one, "or if simply performing RT while at energy balance is sufficient". Source: [Helms 2023 preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-3184470/v1)
- Deficit side: avoid prolonged deficits when the goal is lean mass (Murphy & Koehler). Source: [abstract](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.14075)
- Data-driven practitioner defaults (MacroFactor, October 2024; rates were raised for beginners and intermediates compared with before, still "slow and steady" for most): "happy medium" 0.5 %/week beginner, 0.325 % intermediate, 0.15 % experienced; "conservative" 0.2 / 0.15 / 0.1 % with little or no fat gain and possible recomposition; long-term advice: spend as much time as possible in neutral to positive energy balance. Source: [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)
- Stronger by Science: bulking about 0.1 %/week (experienced) or 0.25 %/week (inexperienced); recomposition between −0.5 and +0.1 %/week depending on body fat and training status. Source: [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/)
- Body fat as a criterion: ISSN: "The higher the baseline body fat level, the more aggressively the caloric deficit may be imposed"; recomposition is most feasible with high body fat and low training age; no evidence-based body-fat window for bulking exists. Sources: [ISSN 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y), [Stronger by Science, deficit](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-caloric-deficit/), [Stronger by Science, p-ratio](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/p-ratios-rebuttal-2/)
- Sex: women are under-researched (ISSN: "paucity of research on women"); the one mixed-sex surplus trial found similar gains in both sexes at +500 kcal/day. Sources: [ISSN 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y), [Sanchez 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38537251/)

### Inferences: what a well founded "gain" goal could look like (my synthesis, numbers a calculation can use)

- Evidence grading: **Well supported**: resistance training plus protein of about 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg is the driver; a deficit impairs lean gain; larger surpluses (15 % or about 500 kcal and more) add mainly fat in trained people; novices and people with higher body fat gain muscle without a surplus. **Theory**: tissue energy cost of about 1 500 to 1 800 kcal/kg muscle; the size of the "insurance" surplus. **Contested or open**: whether a small surplus (5 to 10 %) beats exact maintenance in trained lifters; whether body fat level changes partitioning; optimal rates for women; anything beyond 12 weeks.
- Verdict on the owner's belief: "No surplus needed" is correct for novices, detrained and higher-body-fat users; plausible but unproven for trained lean users (best current reading: little or no measurable loss at maintenance over 8 weeks, but sustained maintenance at a fixed body weight eventually caps total lean mass, and accidental deficits cost gains); "only a very small surplus" matches the direction of the evidence for trained users. It is not the consensus of the position stands and reviews, which still say a surplus is needed to _maximise_ hypertrophy; the consensus is "small surplus, sized by training status", not "none".
- Suggested rate options, as % of body weight per week instead of fixed kg, with an absolute cap (MacroFactor's caps are a usable precedent):
  - Recomposition / "maingaining": 0 to +0.1 %/week (maintenance to about +100 kcal/day). Default for beginners with higher body fat and for returners.
  - Lean gain: novice 0.25 to 0.5 %/week; intermediate 0.15 to 0.33 %/week; advanced 0.1 to 0.25 %/week.
  - Fast gain (explicitly more fat): novice up to 0.8 %/week; intermediate up to 0.5 to 0.6 %; advanced up to 0.35 %.
- In kcal for an 80 kg person at 7 700 kcal/kg: 0.1 % = 88 kcal/day; 0.25 % = 220; 0.5 % = 440. At 5 500 kcal/kg: 63; 157; 314. For a 60 kg person at 7 700: 66; 165; 330. As percent of a 2 700 kcal maintenance these are about 3 %, 8 % and 16 %, matching Iraki's 5 to 20 % and Helms' 5 % and 15 % arms.
- Compared with this, the app's fixed +0.25 kg/week is reasonable for novice and intermediate users of 70 kg and more, too fast for advanced or light users; +0.5 kg/week (+550 kcal) is defensible only for novices or underweight users who accept fat gain (it equals 0.5 %/week only at 100 kg). A slower option of about 0.1 to 0.15 %/week (about +100 to +150 kcal/day) is missing and is the one the newest trials support for trained users.
- Because maintenance estimates are off by more than these surpluses, the surplus should be treated as a start value and adjusted from the weight trend (the app already has an expenditure estimate from own data); the target rate, not the kcal constant, is the quantity to get right.
- Training status could be asked directly (months of consistent training, or MacroFactor's operational definition by rate of strength progress); sex need not change the percentage rate given the lack of data, the percent scaling already yields smaller absolute rates for lighter people.

### Gaps

- No position stand newer than ISSN 2017 on this specific question was found; no 2023 to 2026 meta-analysis of surplus size.
- The terms "maingaining" and "lean bulk" are practitioner vocabulary; no trial tests long-term (6 to 12 months) maintenance vs small surplus in trained lifters.
- MASS Research Review and Examine pages were not opened individually (Examine's FAQ "Is an energy surplus required to gain muscle" exists at https://examine.com/faq/is-an-energy-surplus-required-to-gain-muscle but was not read).
- Details not verified against full texts are marked "Secondary" or "unverified" above: Aragon & Schoenfeld 2020 numbers, Barakat 2020 conditions, Helms 2023 group means, Bray 2012 lean mass per group, Ribeiro's moderate-energy value, McDonald's and Aragon's original tables.
